Your unit economics are the math behind your offer. What does it cost to get a customer? What does it cost to do the work? How much do they pay you, and when do you actually receive that money?

I do not think there is any part of creating an offer more important than this.

Your industry determines a lot of the potential of your business. Your offer affects how easy it is to get someone to buy. Your unit economics determine whether you can keep acquiring and serving customers without eventually running out of money.

You can have something people want, sell it successfully, and still build a business that becomes harder to operate every time you get another customer.

That is what we need to avoid.

Throughout this section, we are talking about your front-end offer. This is the initial offer you sell to someone coming from your marketing. You may have an upsell or a recurring service afterwards, but we want to understand whether the first purchase works on its own.

### Cash Flow vs. Profit

It is a rite of passage as a business owner the first time you have an offer that is profitable while also being cash-flow negative.

If you have no idea what that means, we are going to go through it carefully.

Everyone understands that a business needs to make a profit. If you collect more money than it costs you to do the work, you have something left over. If it costs more than you collect, you have a problem.

But there is another question that becomes extremely important when you try to grow:

When do you actually get the cash?

Let's say it costs you $5,000 to acquire and fulfill for a customer. Over the course of the relationship, they pay you $10,000.

Looking at those two numbers, the customer produces $5,000 above their acquisition and fulfillment costs.

That looks good.

Now suppose you have to pay the $5,000 at the beginning, but the customer pays you $1,000 per month for ten months.

You still expect to collect $10,000 eventually. The total price and total costs have not changed.

But immediately after making the sale, this is your position:

- You have spent $5,000.
- You have collected $1,000.
- You have a $4,000 cash gap.

The future payments do not give you money to spend today.

You have already had to fund the advertising, sales costs, and work required to serve the customer. Getting paid over the next ten months means you have to carry that difference in the meantime.

Now imagine acquiring 100 customers on those same terms.

In this example, where the costs are paid at the beginning:

- Acquisition and fulfillment costs: $500,000.
- First payments collected: $100,000.
- Initial cash gap: $400,000.

You may have sold a million dollars of eventual revenue. You also need $400,000 to cover the initial difference.

This is how an offer can look profitable while growth puts pressure on your bank account. Every new customer creates another gap that someone has to fund.

The offer can work over the full payment period and still be difficult to scale with the money you currently have.

### Cash Collected Means Cash Collected

When we evaluate this, we need to use the amount actually collected when the person buys.

You will hear someone say they just closed a $30,000 deal. That could mean several different things.

If the customer paid $30,000 upfront, they collected $30,000.

If the agreement is $2,500 per month for twelve months, they collected $2,500 from the first payment.

The agreement may be worth $30,000 over its full term, but the initial cash collected is $2,500. That is the number we use for the front-end calculation.

This distinction matters because it changes whether the sale covers its own costs.

Suppose acquiring and fulfilling for that customer costs $5,000. A $30,000 contract sounds extremely profitable compared with that cost. But if the first payment is $2,500, the initial sale has only collected half of what is required to acquire and serve the customer.

You need to fund the other half.

So when we talk about improving the economics of the front-end offer, we have to consider both the price and the payment terms.

A higher total price does not automatically solve the immediate cash problem if the additional money will arrive much later.

For this framework, write down what the customer actually pays at the beginning. Future payments can be valuable, but they belong in your understanding of the future.

### Breaking Even Is A Good First Goal

The next position we want to reach is where the first payment covers acquisition and fulfillment.

Let's use the same $5,000 cost.

This time, the customer pays $5,000 upfront.

$5,000 collected ÷ $5,000 in acquisition and fulfillment costs = 1X.

You have collected enough to cover the cost of getting and serving that customer.

That is a meaningful improvement over collecting $1,000 and having to fund the remaining $4,000 yourself.

But we need to understand exactly what this position gives us.

All $5,000 is needed for this customer. There is no additional money from the sale available to fund the next customer before other business expenses are considered.

You have covered the acquisition and fulfillment costs. You have not created an additional pool of cash for growth.

At a small scale, this can look manageable. You put money into acquiring a customer, collect enough to replace it, and repeat.

The difficulty becomes more obvious when you try to increase how much you are spending.

### Why Breaking Even Still Makes Aggressive Growth Difficult

There is a delay between spending money and getting it back.

You can spend money on advertising today without collecting a customer's payment today. Someone may need to watch your content, complete an application, attend a call, and make a decision.

That takes time.

In the transcript, I talk about this delay potentially being days or weeks. A more involved sales process can take longer. You need enough money available to keep operating while the money you have already spent works its way through that process.

People usually call this "floating" the money.

Suppose your offer breaks even on acquisition and fulfillment. Each completed sale eventually replaces the amount needed for that customer.

That does not mean every dollar you spend is immediately available to spend again.

As you increase your advertising, you can have more money sitting between the point where it has been spent and the point where the customer pays you.

It is one thing to float $10,000, $25,000, or even $100,000, depending on the size of your business. It is another thing to float a million dollars.

At some point, the amount you need to carry can become the thing that stops you.

You may be getting sales. You may be recovering the acquisition and fulfillment costs on those sales. But you cannot increase spending at the rate you want because you do not have enough available cash to carry the larger sales pipeline.

This is why breaking even is a good milestone, but it is not the end goal for the kind of growth we are trying to build.

We want each sale to contribute money towards acquiring the next customer.

### Our Goal Is To Collect At Least 2X Acquisition And Fulfillment Costs

The target in this course is:

Cash collected upfront ÷ (Acquisition cost + Fulfillment cost) = 2X or more.

The brackets matter. We are adding acquisition and fulfillment together, then comparing the cash collected with that combined cost.

If acquiring and fulfilling for a customer costs $5,000, our target is to collect at least $10,000 upfront.

That gives us:

- $5,000 to cover the acquisition and fulfillment costs of this customer.
- Another $5,000 before other business expenses.

At the same cost per customer, that additional $5,000 is enough to acquire and fulfill for the next customer.

When the next customer pays $10,000, the same thing happens again.

This is what I mean by self-funding growth.

The sale covers its own acquisition and fulfillment and contributes enough to fund another customer on the same economics.

You still need to account for the other expenses of running the business, and the sales cycle we just discussed still exists. The difference is that completed sales are now producing additional cash that can support growth.

You are getting money back from the process that can be put into more of the process.

I call this the promised land because it changes the position you are operating from. You have a front-end offer that can help pay for its own expansion.

The target is at least 2X. If you can do better while continuing to provide a good service, that gives you even more room.

If you only remember one calculation from this section, remember this one:

Cash collected upfront ÷ (Acquisition cost + Fulfillment cost).

We want to work towards 2X or more.

### Include The Full Cost Of Acquiring And Serving The Customer

The calculation is only useful if the costs you put into it represent the work you actually have to do.

Acquisition cost is what it costs to get the customer to buy. Depending on your process, that can include advertising, sales commissions, and other costs involved in turning a prospect into a paying customer.

Fulfillment cost is what it costs to deliver the offer they purchased.

That includes the work you still owe them after collecting the money.

Getting paid upfront does not make the remaining work free. If someone pays for a package today and you deliver it over the following weeks, the cost of completing that package still belongs in the calculation.

This is particularly important when you look at your bank account immediately after a sale. Some of that money is already needed for work you have agreed to deliver.

We want to know whether the first payment covers acquiring the customer and fulfilling the entire front-end offer.

We also need to avoid counting the same cost twice. Something included in acquisition should not appear again in fulfillment.

Later, in the worked example, you will see two different types of advertising spend. One gets the client to become our customer. The other produces the leads we have sold to that client.

Those are separate costs doing separate jobs.

### Very Few People Will Start At 2X

This is the goal we are working towards. Very few people will have it perfectly arranged on their first attempt.

There is a progression.

First, get someone to buy the offer at all.

A person who did not already know you sees the offer, understands it, and pays you. That is a real milestone. It gives you something to work with.

Then get to the point where the cash collected covers acquisition and fulfillment.

At 1X, the first sale is covering those costs. You have improved the initial cash position, even though you have not reached the full target.

From there, we keep working on three parts of the calculation:

- Reducing fulfillment cost.
- Improving customer acquisition.
- Increasing what we collect.

These improvements can happen together.

You get better at delivering the service. You improve the marketing and sales process. You accumulate proof and become more confident about the result you can deliver.

Eventually, those changes can bring your front-end cash collected to 2X or more of the combined costs.

The progression is:

Get someone to buy → cover acquisition and fulfillment → improve costs and pricing → reach 2X or more → increase volume.

The first sale matters. Breaking even matters. We are trying to build on those milestones until the economics give us more freedom to grow.

### Reducing Fulfillment Cost

Every time you fulfill the same offer, you have an opportunity to get better at fulfilling it.

You learn which parts take too long. You discover where mistakes happen. You find work that can be templated, documented, or handled through technology.

Those improvements should make their way into how you deliver the next order.

If you solve a problem once but everybody has to solve it again for themselves, you have not gained much from the experience. If you turn the solution into a process or reusable asset, it can keep helping you.

This is where standard operating procedures, training, templates, automation, and AI become useful.

The purpose is to reduce the cost and effort required to produce the result you sold.

As fulfillment becomes more efficient, the same price can leave more money available after the work is done.

You can improve the economics without needing to find a completely different offer or promise a bigger result.

A lot of the value comes from repeatedly doing the same work well enough that you understand how to make it easier.

### Improving Customer Acquisition

Acquisition cost can improve as your ads, funnel, offer, and sales process improve.

You may get more of the right people to click. More of those people may apply or book. More booked prospects may attend. More of the people who attend may buy.

The combined effect determines what it costs to acquire a customer.

That is why we need to look at the whole process. A change that helps someone move through one part of the funnel can affect the final cost of making the sale.

But there is something to remember as you increase volume.

Your acquisition cost will not necessarily keep falling.

As you spend more, your advertising can reach broader audiences. The next group of people may be harder to convert than the people you reached first. Your improvements may help offset that increase without producing a lower acquisition cost than you had at a smaller scale.

We should keep trying to improve acquisition. We should also use the costs we are actually experiencing.

The economics need to work at the level where you want to operate. A cost you achieved on a small amount of spend does not tell you what every additional customer will cost forever.

### Increasing Your Price

As you accumulate proof and get better at producing results, you can become more confident about charging more.

You understand the work better. You have evidence of what you can do. You have heard more of the questions and objections people raise before buying.

If demand begins filling your available capacity, that also gives you room to test a higher price.

An increase in price can improve the calculation without requiring the same increase in fulfillment cost.

That matters because we are trying to increase what we collect while controlling what it costs to acquire and serve the customer.

The pricing rule in this framework is to try increasing the price by 20% every four customers, provided the close rate remains above 20%.

For example, a 20% increase on a $5,000 price takes it to $6,000.

This is a rule of thumb to test and evaluate. We need to see what happens to sales and acquisition cost after the change.

The objective is to improve the complete calculation. We care about what we collect, what it takes to get the sale, and what the work costs to deliver.

### The Supplemental Goals Help You Diagnose The Problem

The 2X cash-collected target is the main financial test in this framework.

I compare this with customer acquisition cost in advertising. If you are acquiring customers at a cost that works for the business, an individual advertising metric looking less impressive does not automatically mean the campaign is failing.

The same principle applies here.

We have secondary targets that can help us work out what needs attention. They are especially useful when we are not reaching 2X.

The targets in this course are:

- 80% gross margin.
- $250 or more in revenue per fulfillment hour.
- 6–10% application rate.
- 3–5% qualified booked-call rate.
- 50–70% show rate.
- 20–25% close rate.

These are working benchmarks for diagnosing the offer and funnel. They help us decide where to look.

They are not six separate tests that automatically overrule the main calculation. The worked example demonstrates this: it reaches 2X with a 70% gross margin.

### Understanding The Gross Margin Target

For this framework, we are looking at what remains from the sale after the direct fulfillment costs.

Gross margin = (Revenue − Fulfillment cost) ÷ Revenue.

An 80% gross margin means fulfillment uses 20% of the revenue.

If fulfillment takes a large share of what the customer pays, there is less room left for acquiring the customer and covering the other requirements of the business.

That is why gross margin is useful when you are trying to understand why you are not reaching 2X.

You can look at whether the offer requires too much work for the price, whether the delivery process is inefficient, or whether you need to charge more.

But gross margin alone does not tell you whether the whole front-end calculation works. You still have to include acquisition cost and look at cash collected.

A business can have an attractive gross margin and still spend too much getting each customer. Another offer can have a lower gross margin and pass the 2X test because the complete numbers work.

We need to evaluate them together.

### Understanding The Effective Hourly Rate

The $250 target is about how much revenue the offer produces relative to the hours required to fulfill it.

The calculation is:

Offer price ÷ Total fulfillment hours.

If it takes 20 hours to fulfill an offer and you charge $5,000:

$5,000 ÷ 20 = $250 per fulfillment hour.

That is revenue per hour of fulfillment, before the associated costs.

This gives us another way to understand whether we have built too much work into the offer for what we charge.

It also connects directly to capacity. If every customer requires a large number of hours, increasing the number of customers means finding a way to provide all of those hours.

You may need to improve the process, standardize more of the work, or change the price.

Reducing the hours required while maintaining the result can improve both the unit economics and the amount of work your team can handle.

### Understanding The Funnel Benchmarks

The application, booking, show, and close rates help you look at the acquisition side of the calculation.

If people enter the funnel but very few apply or book, we need to understand what is happening before the appointment.

If people book but do not attend, we need to examine the period between booking and the call.

If qualified people attend but very few buy, we need to look at the offer, the sales conversation, and whether those people were appropriate prospects in the first place.

The ranges in this framework give you somewhere to begin that evaluation:

- Application rate: 6–10%.
- Qualified booked-call rate: 3–5%.
- Show rate: 50–70%.
- Close rate: 20–25%.

The purpose of improving these numbers is to improve the cost of acquiring a customer.

You can have a page that looks good and a sales process you feel confident about, but the actual numbers may show where people are dropping out.

We then work on the part that is affecting the result.

##### Once The Economics Work, Capacity Becomes The Next Problem

When the offer starts producing enough cash to support further acquisition, your ability to fulfill the work becomes much more important.

You may have the money to acquire another customer. You still need the capacity to serve them.

Most service businesses are made up of people, and people can only complete so much work in the time available.

This is one reason software can be an attractive business model. A large part of the delivery happens through the software itself. Each additional customer does not necessarily create the same amount of manual work.

Our service business may be different. More customers may require more people or more available hours.

So the next question becomes how we increase delivery capacity without making the business unnecessarily complicated.

This brings us back to the way we constructed the offer.

If the work is standardized, we can build training, processes, and technology around delivering the same thing repeatedly.

If each new customer requires a substantially different service, increasing capacity becomes harder. You keep adding work that does not fit what your people and processes are already set up to handle.

Good economics give you the ability to fund growth. Standardized fulfillment helps you handle the work that growth brings.

### Why The Front-End Offer Needs To Be Standardized

For the model we are building, the front-end offer needs to be standardized.

You can choose to do custom work in an upsell or a separate back-end service. But the initial offer we are trying to acquire customers for repeatedly should have a clear, repeatable delivery process.

There are three reasons this matters to the economics.

First, repeated fulfillment allows improvements to carry forward.

When you keep delivering the same offer, the processes you build can help with the next customer. The training becomes more useful. The templates and assets have somewhere to be used again.

If the fulfillment changes substantially every time, much of that improvement stays attached to the individual project.

Second, repeated selling allows the acquisition process to improve around a consistent offer.

You can improve the ads, pages, and sales conversations because you know what you are selling. The objections you learn to answer remain relevant to future prospects.

If the offer changes on every call, you have a harder time understanding whether the marketing process itself is improving. You are repeatedly asking it to sell something different.

Third, repeated results give you relevant proof and confidence.

You have done the same work for similar customers. You understand what it involves and have a better basis for explaining what a new customer can expect.

That helps you sell and gives you more confidence when testing a higher price.

Standardization supports both sides of the calculation: controlling costs and increasing what you can collect.

### Why Your Industry Matters Here

This also connects to the earlier discussion about industries and non-industries.

If your customers have very little in common, you may find that each customer requires a different approach. The work that helped one customer does not transfer cleanly to the next.

That makes it harder to build the repeated improvements we need.

You want your experience to become useful beyond the customer you gained it from. A process you improve should have a reasonable chance of helping with the next order.

The same applies to proof. Results from a similar customer are easier to use when explaining the offer to someone new.

Serving a broad group of unrelated businesses can make this more difficult because the work, circumstances, and relevant evidence may keep changing.

This is one of the reasons the Core Seven cannot be separated completely. Your industry and ICP influence how much you can standardize. Standardization affects fulfillment cost, acquisition, and your confidence in the price.

Those decisions eventually show up in the unit economics.

### Get As Close To "Selling Air" As Possible

The mindset here is to get as close to "selling air" as possible.

By that, I mean increasing what we collect while reducing the additional cost and effort required to fulfill another order.

The customer still needs to receive something valuable. We are trying to become extremely efficient at providing it.

Software and digital advertising illustrate the idea. Once the infrastructure exists, it can support additional customers without rebuilding the entire product for each one.

There are still costs. What matters is how much additional cost appears when another customer buys.

Our service may never have economics quite like that. But we can work in the same direction.

We can build reusable assets, improve our processes, and use technology to reduce work that otherwise has to be repeated manually.

When an improvement helps every future order, the benefit continues after the original work of making that improvement is finished.

That is why I put so much emphasis on standardization, training, and efficiency. We want the same offer to become easier to deliver as our experience increases.

### The Three Tiers

We can put an offer into one of three tiers using the same calculation:

Cash collected upfront ÷ (Acquisition cost + Fulfillment cost).

The tier tells us how the first payment compares with what it costs to get and serve that customer.

Remember to include the full fulfillment obligation. Work you will complete after collecting payment still has a cost.

### Tier 1: Collecting Less Than 1X

In Tier 1, the cash collected from the first sale does not cover acquisition and fulfillment.

If the combined cost is $2,500, anything below $2,500 collected puts you in this tier.

You have to fund the difference.

This can happen with an offer that is profitable over the full relationship. The payment-plan example belongs here because too little of the money is collected at the beginning.

You may eventually collect enough to produce a good result. At the point of the first sale, however, the customer has not paid enough to cover the costs in this framework.

Your first goal is to get to break-even.

That means working on what you collect upfront and what it costs to acquire and serve the customer until the initial payment covers those costs.

### Tier 2: Collecting At Least 1X, But Less Than 2X

At exactly 1X, the sale covers acquisition and fulfillment.

If those costs are $2,500 and you collect $2,500, you have reached that point.

As you move above 1X, there is more money left after covering those costs. You are improving your position, but you have not yet collected enough to cover this customer and another customer at the same cost.

There is a meaningful difference between being exactly at 1X and approaching 2X, even though both fall within this tier.

It is also perfectly possible to operate a business in Tier 2. You may choose a pace of growth that works with the money the business produces.

The reason we are working towards Tier 3 is to give ourselves more room to fund additional acquisition.

Tier 2 is progress. We keep improving the offer and its delivery until the complete calculation reaches the target.

### Tier 3: Collecting 2X Or More

In Tier 3, the upfront payment is at least twice the combined acquisition and fulfillment costs.

If those costs are $2,500, you collect $5,000 or more.

The first $2,500 covers getting and serving this customer. Another $2,500 remains before other business expenses.

That is enough, at the same economics, to put towards getting and serving the next customer.

This is the target we have been discussing throughout the section.

The money produced by the front-end offer can contribute directly to more front-end acquisition. We then need to make sure the business can continue delivering as volume increases.

### Applying This To A Lead Generation Business

Say we ran a marketing agency that sold qualified, exclusive leads.

The company buying those leads is our customer.

The leads themselves are what we deliver to that customer.

Keeping that distinction clear matters because there are two different types of advertising spend in this example.

We would spend money to acquire the client. We would also spend money generating the leads that client had purchased.

The first is our customer acquisition cost. The second is part of fulfilling our offer.

We are using the following assumptions:

- We sell each qualified, exclusive lead for $100.
- Each lead costs us $25 in advertising to generate.
- It costs $2,000 to acquire the client.
- We allow $500 in labor for the first order, including setup and CRM integration.
- The customer buys 100 leads and pays upfront.

Now we can work through the calculation.

### Cash Collected

The company buys 100 leads at $100 each.

100 × $100 = $10,000.

Because they pay upfront, our cash collected from the first sale is $10,000.

We have also committed to delivering all 100 leads. Collecting the money before completing delivery does not remove that commitment from our costs.

### Fulfillment Cost

Generating the 100 leads costs:

100 × $25 = $2,500.

We also allow $500 in labor for setup and delivery work associated with the first order.

So fulfillment is:

$2,500 in lead-generation ad spend + $500 in labor = $3,000.

This is what it costs, under the example's assumptions, to deliver the package the client purchased.

### Customer Acquisition Cost

It costs us $2,000 to acquire the client.

That is separate from the $2,500 spent generating their leads.

Our combined cost is therefore:

$2,000 to acquire the client + $3,000 to fulfill the order = $5,000.

Now we have both numbers required for the framework.

### The 2X Calculation

Cash collected: $10,000.

Acquisition and fulfillment costs: $5,000.

$10,000 ÷ $5,000 = 2X.

The offer reaches Tier 3.

After those acquisition and fulfillment costs, $5,000 remains before the other expenses of running the business.

At the same economics, that amount can fund acquiring another client and fulfilling another initial order.

This is the complete example of what we have been trying to construct: an upfront purchase that covers its own costs and contributes enough to fund another customer.

##### Why The First Order Is 100 Leads

The size of the first order affects the economics.

We have $2,000 in customer acquisition cost and $500 in labor allowed for the first order. In this model, that is $2,500 before the advertising required to generate the leads themselves.

Each lead produces $100 in revenue and costs $25 to generate.

That leaves $75 per lead to cover the $2,500 and then contribute beyond it.

To find where we begin covering those costs:

$2,500 ÷ $75 = 33.33 leads.

We cannot sell a fraction of a lead, so the first whole number that covers the costs is 34.

At 34 leads:

- Cash collected: 34 × $100 = $3,400.
- Lead-generation ad spend: 34 × $25 = $850.
- Labor: $500.
- Client acquisition cost: $2,000.
- Combined acquisition and fulfillment costs: $3,350.
- Amount remaining before other business expenses: $50.

So 34 leads gets us just above break-even on acquisition and fulfillment.

That is very different from reaching 2X.

To find the order size required for 2X, we compare the revenue with twice the costs.

For an order of N leads:

Cash collected = $100 × N.

Acquisition and fulfillment costs = $2,500 + ($25 × N).

Our target is:

$100 × N = 2 × [$2,500 + ($25 × N)].

That becomes:

$100 × N = $5,000 + ($50 × N).

So:

$50 × N = $5,000.

N = 100 leads.

This is why the example uses an initial purchase of 100 leads.

At 34 leads, we are only just covering the modeled costs. At 100 leads, we collect $10,000 against $5,000 in acquisition and fulfillment costs.

The purchase size helps determine whether the first order reaches the financial target.

##### Why This Example Works At A 70% Gross Margin

Our cash collected is $10,000, and the direct fulfillment cost is $3,000.

Gross margin is:

($10,000 − $3,000) ÷ $10,000 = 70%.

That is below the supplemental 80% gross-margin target.

But when we include the $2,000 customer acquisition cost, the complete calculation still reaches 2X:

$10,000 ÷ ($2,000 + $3,000) = 2X.

So the offer passes the main test.

This is why we need to understand the purpose of the supplemental goals. The 80% gross-margin target helps us diagnose and improve the business. It does not mean an offer that reaches the 2X target at 70% automatically fails.

We can continue working on delivery efficiency and pricing. But under the assumptions in this example, the initial order already covers its acquisition and fulfillment costs and leaves the amount required for another customer before other business expenses.

That is the position we are trying to build.
