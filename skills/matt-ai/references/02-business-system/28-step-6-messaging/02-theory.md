Messaging is where the decisions we have made about the business become something a customer can see.

Your industry, ICP, offer, and Big Idea can all be sensible. If you communicate them badly, the marketing can still perform badly.

People have to recognize that you are talking to them. They have to understand why the problem matters and how your offer relates to it.

This is one of the biggest reasons businesses attract the wrong leads. They intend to sell to one type of customer while their words appeal to another.

Then they spend all their time changing targeting settings or complaining about lead quality without examining the message that attracted those people.

We need to get the person, the problem, and the language right.

### Messaging Helps Determine Who You Attract

The customer makes a decision about relevance before they fill out your application.

If the ad describes a problem they have, they may pay attention. If it describes someone else's situation, they may scroll past it.

This is why your words are part of your targeting.

“Get your first client” is a very different invitation from “build a sales team that can close without you.” Both might appear in marketing aimed at agency owners, but they speak to different levels of business.

Ad platforms also use signals from creative, responses, and conversion events to decide how to deliver advertising. The exact behavior depends on the platform and setup, but the practical relationship matters: the people who respond influence what your results and optimization data look like.

Messaging → response → conversion signals → further delivery.

If your message repeatedly attracts the wrong customer, you can keep generating more of the wrong signals.

The words do not control everything. Your offer, targeting, tracking, and qualification still matter. But you should not treat messaging as decoration added after the important acquisition decisions have been made.

### You Already Have Messaging

Whether you realize it or not, you already have messaging. Something in your paid ads, content, outreach, or funnel caused people to book a sales call. The quality of those people tells you how well it is working.

So before writing anything new, look at what the current message has already brought you.

Pull your last 100 sales calls that came from paid ads or outreach. If you have fewer than that, use all of them. If you have none at all, there is nothing to audit and you go straight to the research.

Then score every call against four tests. Did the prospect match your ICP characteristics? Were they financially qualified? Did they have decision-making power? Did they buy within seven days?

You are looking for at least 80% on the first three and at least 50% on the last one.

Pass zero or one of those tests and your messaging is consistently attracting the wrong people. Pass two or three and part of it is working while something major is not. Pass all four and it is already doing its job.

Either way, you still do the research afterward. A good score tells you what you already have. It does not tell you what else the market is saying.

### Describe Their World Accurately

In the ICP section, we identified problems, circumstances, and desired outcomes.

Now we need to understand how the customer actually describes them.

“Customer acquisition difficulties” might be a technically accurate category. It is probably not the sentence the owner says on a sales call.

They might say, “Every time I try to increase the budget, my cost per customer goes through the roof.”

That gives you more information. They already advertise. The ads work at some level. The problem appears when they try to increase spending.

You can see the difference between that and a beginner saying, “I have never run an ad and do not know where to start.”

Both could be put in a spreadsheet under advertising problems. They should not automatically become the same message.

The detail tells the right customer that you understand their situation.

### Do Not Guess. Do Research.

Your best source is often your own sales call recordings.

People explain what is wrong, what they have tried, what they are worried about, and what they want instead. They also show you how they describe those things when they are speaking naturally.

If you have hundreds of recordings, a large amount of the information you need may already be there.

You can supplement that with customer emails, reviews, forums, Reddit, Quora, and other places the intended customer discusses the problem.

But keep the research inside your ICP. A review written by someone in the right industry is not automatically a review written by the right level of customer.

If you combine beginners, established operators, employees, and business owners into one undifferentiated list, you can recreate the exact problem the research was supposed to solve.

Know whose words you are using.

### Preserve The Language When You Use AI

AI can help organize a large amount of research, group recurring issues, and identify how frequently a problem appears.

The useful part is the sorting. Do not let it polish away the language you collected.

You want the actual phrases and situations, alongside your understanding of the problem. If everything becomes “seeking scalable solutions to improve efficiency,” you have lost most of what made the research valuable.

Keep a distinction between an actual customer quote, your summary, and a draft line you wrote for an ad.

They have different jobs. The quote tells you what someone said. The summary helps you organize it. The draft is something you still need to test.

### What I Learned In Auto Insurance

For years, much of my auto insurance advertising was some version of saving money. The offer often appeared as insurance starting at a particular monthly price.

Then I went through public reviews for large insurance companies and manually sorted what people complained about.

In that set of reviews, price was not even in the top 15 issues I found.

The complaints were more like:

- They were helpful during the sales process, then disappeared when I needed them.
- They offered too little on my claim.
- The claims process took forever.
- I paid on time for years, and when I finally needed them, they let me down.

That was a very different picture from the one I had been using in the ads.

I had assumed that the primary problem was price. The research showed other reasons people were unhappy enough to consider a change.

I started making ads about those problems and using the language people had used to describe them. One complaint could produce many different executions because different people expressed it in different ways.

The change helped me expand the advertising substantially. In my experience, the business grew roughly tenfold over the following four months.

I had spent years assuming I already knew what customers cared about. The complaints were sitting there in public reviews the whole time.

### A Problem And An Ad Angle Are Related

An angle is the particular way you approach the customer's attention.

You might lead with the problem itself, the situation around it, or the outcome they want.

For example:

- Problem: “My ads do not work.”
- Circumstance: “I have tried everything to get my ads working and nothing has helped.”
- Outcome: “I want to get customers through paid ads.”

Those statements are related. They are not identical.

The second includes a history of frustration. The third starts with a desired outcome. The person may respond differently depending on what they have experienced and what is on their mind when they see the ad.

Do not combine similar angles so quickly that you remove useful distinctions before testing them.

You can have one broad problem and several meaningful ways to talk about it.

### In-Market And Out-Of-Market Buyers

Not every suitable customer is looking to buy right now.

Some are actively searching for the service. These are in-market buyers.

Others might already have a provider, handle the work internally, have different priorities, or simply be comfortable waiting. They can fit your ICP while being out of market for the offer at that moment.

That is important because a person ignoring a direct offer does not automatically mean they are the wrong customer.

They may understand it and believe it is useful without being ready to make a change.

The broad percentages people use to explain this concept vary by market. You do not need to assume a fixed percentage for every business. You need to recognize that the group ready to buy today is smaller than the entire group you could eventually serve.

Your marketing needs to work with that timing.

### People Who Are Not Shopping Still Have Problems

A business owner can have a problem without being ready to hire someone to fix it.

They might be trying to solve it themselves. They might be tied to an existing provider. Another problem may be more urgent this month.

Useful messaging still gives them a reason to pay attention.

You explain a situation they recognize. You show them something they can try. You demonstrate how another company dealt with it.

Over time, those encounters can help you become someone they remember when they are ready to act.

This does not mean repetition guarantees a sale. It means your marketing has a job beyond asking the same immediate buying question over and over.

We want to be relevant before the person begins actively comparing providers.

### Start With A Direct Offer

The easiest place to begin is usually the person who already wants what you sell.

Tell them what it is, who it is for, show relevant proof, and explain the next step.

You do not need to make everything clever.

For a hypothetical roofing lead generation business, the direct offer could be:

"Residential roofing companies with three or more crews: get qualified, exclusive leads on a pay-per-lead basis. Get your first lead within 48 hours."

It identifies the buyer, describes the deliverable, explains the pricing basis, and gives a time to first value.

You would need to support those claims and define the relevant terms on the page. But the basic message is easy to understand.

If someone is already looking for that service, a direct explanation may be exactly what they need.

### A Direct Offer Can Take You A Long Way

Depending on the market and the economics, you may be able to build a substantial acquisition system around a fairly direct offer.

That is why I would start there before creating dozens of complicated campaigns.

You learn whether people want the offer, whether the funnel converts, and whether the customer is worth acquiring at the resulting cost.

As you increase spending, watch whether you are still getting customers profitably. I like gradual increases of around 20% per week while the economics hold. Let the actual results determine whether you keep increasing.

Eventually, increasing spend may stop producing enough additional customers at acceptable costs.

That is when broader messaging can help you reach more of the market. More budget on the same limited approach may not solve the problem.

### Horizontal And Vertical Scaling

In this course, I use horizontal scaling to mean creating versions of the message for different types of people inside the broader market.

I use vertical scaling to mean reaching more of the same ICP through different problems, circumstances, and desired outcomes.

Both can be useful. The right place to focus depends on the customer and how broad the market is.

The distinction helps you identify what is changing in the test: who sees themselves in the execution, or which part of the customer's world the message addresses.

### Horizontal Scaling

For a broad offer, you can keep the central promise while changing the examples, people, locations, or situations used to present it.

Different members of the market may recognize themselves in different executions.

That can mean using different customer stories or having different people explain the same offer. It can mean showing the service in a situation that is familiar to another part of the market.

The product does not necessarily change.

The question is whether another execution makes the same useful offer more relevant to people it could already serve.

Treat those versions as tests. The appearance of the person in an ad does not guarantee the characteristics of the audience that will buy.

For a very narrow B2B ICP, there may be more value in going deeper into the customer's problems before making lots of variations based on broad demographic differences.

### Vertical Scaling

Suppose there are 10,000 people in your ICP, and, for this example, 3% are actively looking for the service.

That gives you 300 people who may be immediately receptive to a direct offer.

The remaining 9,700 can still have relevant problems. They simply are not all thinking, “I need to buy this service now.”

Vertical scaling gives them other reasons to pay attention.

An agency owner might ignore an ad selling marketing help and pay attention to a detailed explanation of why their sales rep cannot close as well as they can. Another may care about diversifying acquisition before selling the company.

They can both be the right customer. You are approaching different parts of their situation.

This is where a strong research base becomes useful. You need more than one generic description of the industry's main problem.

### Stay Inside The Same ICP

Expanding your angles should not accidentally change the customer.

If you want established business owners, a message about making the first sale may bring you a larger audience while making the leads less useful to your offer.

That is not the expansion we are trying to create.

Go deeper into what your intended customer deals with. Look at their team, current acquisition methods, constraints, ambitions, and past attempts to solve the problem.

The message can change while the underlying customer remains consistent.

This is also why research needs to be checked before it becomes an ad. A common problem in a large dataset may be common among people outside your ICP.

### Workhorse Angles

Over time, you want a few angles that can support a meaningful amount of profitable acquisition.

I call these workhorse angles.

They usually address problems or outcomes that matter to a large share of your ICP. That gives you room to create many different ads under the same theme.

In the course, I use roughly four as a useful way to think about the goal. The important thing is finding several proven angles, not stopping because you reached a particular count.

One angle can have different openings, examples, customer stories, demonstrations, and explanations.

That gives you room to keep developing the creative without constantly changing the underlying promise or customer.

### Niche Angles

Other angles may speak strongly to a smaller group.

A very particular situation could matter enormously to someone experiencing it while meaning little to most of the market.

That can still be useful.

Someone might ignore several broad ads and finally respond to a specific description of the problem they are trying to solve this week.

A niche angle may support less spend than a workhorse angle. That does not make it a bad angle if the customers it acquires are worthwhile.

This is why we separate the quality of an angle from the size of its audience. A precise, valuable message can be strong without being capable of carrying the whole account.

### Your Angles Work Together

People can encounter your business more than once and through more than one channel.

One ad discusses lead flow. Another explains a sales problem. A video shows the work. An email answers a concern. A case study gives evidence.

Those encounters can reinforce one another.

The Big Idea keeps them connected. Different problems lead back to a consistent explanation of what matters and how you help.

We want the customer to recognize that you understand their world. When the timing changes and they want help, that familiarity can make you an obvious business to consider.

The content still needs to be useful and the claims still need support. Frequency cannot make an irrelevant message relevant by itself.

### The Language Changes With The Customer's Level

Different customers in the same industry can use different terms because they are managing different problems.

A smaller ecommerce business might talk mostly about return on ad spend. A more established team might discuss contribution margin, payback periods, or whether the advertising is creating additional sales it would not otherwise have received.

You do not need jargon to sound sophisticated. You need to understand what those customers actually measure and worry about.

Using a technical term without understanding the problem behind it will not help much when the person asks a follow-up question.

The aim is accurate familiarity. Their language should make sense because you understand the work and circumstances it describes.

That is one reason language also becomes a form of proof in the next lesson.

### How We Organize The Research

The research can begin with roughly 30 recurring problems, then expand into 50 or more angles as you include circumstances, outcomes, and different ways of describing them.

Those are different counts. You are not required to discover 50 entirely unrelated problems.

For each problem, record how customers describe it, how often it appears, and whether it connects naturally to your Big Idea.

Also notice intensity. A frequent complaint can be minor, while a less frequent problem can be urgent and expensive for the people facing it.

Use the information to decide what deserves testing. The research gives you informed candidates; campaign results tell you how those candidates perform.

### The Five Checks For An Angle

We evaluate each angle against five things:

1. Does it match the exact ICP, including their level?
2. Does it address a real problem they care about?
3. Does it use language they naturally use?
4. Does it connect to the Big Idea?
5. Does it make the offer relevant?

Each receives a score from one to five, for a total of 25.

The framework labels 23–25 excellent, 20–22 good, 16–19 neutral, 11–15 poor, and 5–10 bad.

The scores help you inspect the thinking before putting money behind it. They do not replace a test of the actual message.

An angle can receive a strong score and still need a better execution. It can also be high quality while applying to a relatively small number of customers.

### Why These Roofing Messages Speak To Different Buyers

Consider three examples:

“Do you need more lead flow to fill your sales team's calendar?”

“Are you at risk of missing your quarterly revenue targets?”

“Are you looking to diversify your lead sources?”

These suggest a company with an existing sales operation, planning process, or established acquisition channels.

Compare that with a message focused on getting the phone to ring for the first time.

The subject is still leads. The implied business situation is different.

These phrases are signals, not perfect filters. A small business can have targets and a large business can have a quiet phone. The point is to choose language that accurately describes the customer you intend to reach.
