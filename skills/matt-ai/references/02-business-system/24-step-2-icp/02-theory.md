Your industry tells us the market you serve. Your ICP tells us who you want to sell to inside that market.

ICP stands for ideal customer profile. Some people say ideal client profile or call it their avatar. The name matters much less than whether you have actually thought about the customer.

A lot of people combine industry and ICP and call the whole thing their niche. I like to separate them because important thinking gets lost when you treat them as the same decision.

"Roofing companies" is an industry.

"The head of marketing at a multi-state roofing company with a call center" describes a much more specific buyer inside it.

Those two descriptions will lead to very different offers, marketing, and sales conversations.

### Everything Is Downstream Of Your ICP

Your offer needs to solve a problem this customer cares about.

Your Big Idea needs to connect with something they believe.

Your messaging needs to sound relevant to their situation.

Your proof needs to help them believe the service can work for someone like them.

Offer, Big Idea, messaging and proof are all covered in the courses that follow this one.

Everything we do from here on out needs to be based on the ICP. If this is not done intentionally, it is very unlikely to be done correctly.

If you get the customer wrong here, you can do a lot of good work on the wrong things later.

You might write a clear ad that attracts people you do not want.

You might create a useful course for customers who cannot afford your service.

You might collect impressive proof that means very little to the person you now want to sell to.

That is why we need more than a general category of people who could theoretically buy.

We need to understand the customer the business is being built around.

### The Level Of Customer Changes The Business

An industry can become much more or less attractive depending on who you choose to serve inside it.

There are some better industries such as finance, health care, and law, and there are some bad ones such as newspapers, restaurants, and realtors. But any neutral industry will depend on who you are targeting inside it.

If you are targeting higher-level people, it is usually quite good. If you accidentally target low-level people, it is usually pretty bad.

Take our roofing example.

You could sell lead generation to a solo owner-operator with a couple of helpers. You could also sell it to a private-equity-backed roofing company operating across several states with a call center and a sales team.

Both businesses install roofs.

Both need customers.

They do not have the same ability to buy, the same capacity to use what you sell, or the same problems.

The owner-operator might be personally answering the phone between jobs. A larger business might have several salespeople who need appointments and crews that need a steady flow of work.

Your service could be useful to both. But the volume they can buy and the systems they already have can make the relationship very different.

For a service business, this affects more than the invoice. It affects how much support the customer needs, what you have to build for them, and how likely they are to turn your work into a result.

We want customers who have a real need for the offer and the ability to benefit from it.

### You Can Scale By Moving Upmarket

People often picture growth as getting more and more customers at the same price.

That is one way to grow. But most skilled white-collar service businesses do not scale by getting thousands of clients at $3,000 per month. They scale by scaling their ICP.

They sell essentially the same thing to wealthier and wealthier clients, increasing their total average value and their profit margins at the same time.

The customer gets more value from the work, can afford to pay more, and may already have the people and systems needed to use it.

Think about the difference between 1,000 customers paying $3,000 per month and 100 customers paying $30,000 per month.

Both produce $3 million in monthly revenue. They create very different businesses to operate.

That does not mean the second business has exactly one-tenth of the costs. A larger customer may have more requirements. The example shows why customer count on its own tells you very little about the quality of the business.

As your amount of proof scales, you should increase your prices and go after larger clients.

You do not have to begin with the largest company in the industry. You do need to understand how the customer you choose affects the business you can build.

### The Customer You Want And The Customer Who Buys

There are two sides to defining your ICP.

You can intentionally choose who you want to serve and build the offer and marketing around them.

You also need to look at who actually buys and what happens after they buy.

The people who buy are the people who buy. Your initial description does not override the evidence.

If you have enough customers, look through the last 100, or the last 1,000 if your business is large enough. Go back about the last six months so the data reflects the current version of the business rather than customers who bought a completely different offer years ago.

You are looking for a small number of characteristics that explain a large part of the customer base.

In the course, I suggest trying to identify two to five shared characteristics that describe roughly 80% of customers.

It will not be 100% perfect. There will always be outliers on either extreme. But if you dive deep into your customer base, you will be able to identify characteristics they share.

We are trying to see the pattern without letting the exceptions become the whole strategy.

### Choose Characteristics That Actually Matter

There are some standard characteristics, and then there are countless niche ones. These are more like characteristics that describe someone. Usually only two to five will be relevant for your ICP.

For a business selling to consumers, the standard list is age, gender, location, race, marital status, homeowner status, kids, household income, and net worth.

For a business selling to other businesses, the standard list is age, gender, location, job title, revenue, employee count, and tech stack.

Then there are characteristics specific to your service.

We want a high-level ICP as our customers, because that allows us to charge more and get a better result. For a lead generation business, that might look like this:

- At least three sales reps.
- Already have a call center.
- Have run ads before.
- Operate in at least three states.

Those details can matter much more than a long description of a fictional customer's hobbies.

The useful test is whether the characteristic changes the buying decision, your ability to reach them, or your ability to get them a result.

You do not need every possible field filled in. You need the few details that explain why this customer is a good fit.

### What My Agency Program Showed Me

In my agency program, I sold to more than 1,800 agency owners over an 18-month period.

When I looked at the customer data, four characteristics described 91% of that group:

- Age: 25–34.
- Location: United States.
- Gender: male.
- Revenue: $500,000–$5 million per year.

That was a pattern in my customer base. It was not a claim that nobody outside those characteristics could buy or benefit.

Is there anything stopping someone outside that age range? No.

Is there anything stopping females from buying? No.

Were there clients outside the USA? Sure.

The revenue range also helped explain the fit. Under $500,000 they may not be able to afford it. Over $5 million they probably do not need it.

But even though there is nothing stopping them from buying, you better believe that when I run ads, I target 25-34 year old male Americans only. It is just how it is.

The lesson for your business is to look at the evidence you actually have. Do not copy the description of my customers and assume it is the right description of yours.

Those characteristics helped me understand who I was selling to. The next part explains what those people actually cared about.

### Problems, Circumstances, And Outcomes

Knowing someone's job title and company size does not tell you what to say to them.

The second thing we want to do with our ICP is find a list of problems they face, circumstances they find themselves in, and outcomes they desire.

- Problems: something is going wrong that they want fixed.
- Circumstances: a situation they currently find themselves in, usually negative.
- Outcomes: something they want to achieve.

These become the material for your offer, Big Idea, and messaging.

The characteristics have three uses. Age, gender and location go straight into your ad targeting. Job title and employee count are what you filter on when you export a cold outreach list. Both of those are fairly minor compared to the third use, which is research. Having the characteristics data first helps us visualize the customer and find the best way to research them.

A characteristic might tell us that the person owns an agency doing $3 million per year. Their circumstances tell us that they are still handling every sales call and cannot get their new closer to perform as well as they do.

Now we have something meaningful to work with.

We know what is happening in their business. We can understand why it matters, what they have probably tried, and what a useful result would look like.

This is where generic research often fails. People research the industry as a whole and combine problems from customers at completely different levels.

Then they build a message that feels relevant to the wrong person.

### Small And Large Agency Owners Have Different Problems

Consider two agency owners.

The first is trying to get established. Their list looks like this:

- Outcome: get your first agency client.
- Outcome: get to $10,000 per month.
- Circumstance: no one takes me seriously without proof.
- Problem: no one responds to my cold emails.
- Problem: I do not have an offer yet.

Those are real problems. They can support useful products and services.

But they are not the problems of every agency owner.

An established agency owner's list looks completely different:

- Circumstance: every time you try to scale past $1,000 per day in ad spend, CPA skyrockets.
- Circumstance: looking to sell your agency in the next two years.
- Outcome: would like to hire a first sales rep and get out of sales.
- Outcome: would like to add a second acquisition channel.
- Problem: closers do not close at the same level as the owner.

Both people own agencies. Almost everything about the offer they would find valuable is different.

If your customer already has a successful agency, "get your first client" gives them no reason to pay attention.

If your customer has never had a client, a detailed explanation of building a sales department may be irrelevant to what they need right now.

This is why the level of ICP matters so much. You can name the right industry while describing the wrong customer's life.

### The Words You Use Reveal The Level You Are Speaking To

Compare two sets of messaging for roofing companies.

Roofing Messaging A:

- "Tired of losing jobs to that roofer down the street?"
- "Tired of sitting around waiting for the phone to ring?"
- "Don't know where your next job is coming from?"
- "Do you need more clients?"

Roofing Messaging B:

- "Do you need more volume for your sales team?"
- "Are you looking to diversify your traffic sources?"
- "Are you about to miss your quarterly revenue projections?"
- "Are you looking to sell to private equity in the next 24 months?"

Which message is more likely to attract beginners? Roofing Messaging A.

Which is more likely to attract large roofing companies? Roofing Messaging B.

This is obvious when you look at the two side by side. Every hour of every day, people make this exact mistake without realizing it.

If we are targeting huge, multi-state roofing companies that can pay us a lot, would they be tired of losing jobs to that roofer down the street? No. They probably would not even know that roofer exists.

Would they be sitting around waiting for the phone to ring? No. They probably have 200 spreadsheets, a CRM, and a call center. Their phone is ringing off the hook.

Would they have no idea where their next job is coming from? No. They probably have about 1,000 jobs happening at this very moment.

Now look at the other side.

If a company needs more volume for its sales team, that implies it already has a sales team and is probably a larger company.

If it is thinking about diversifying its traffic sources and knows why that would be valuable, it is probably larger and more sophisticated.

If it has quarterly revenue projections, it is an organized business with planning processes and systems.

The words you use reveal the level of customer you are speaking to.

### Why This Shows Up As Unqualified Leads

Imagine that you want to sell to established ecommerce businesses, but your ads talk about getting a store's first sale.

Who is most likely to care about that message?

Someone trying to get their first sale.

If those people click, apply, and book, the marketing has attracted people who found the message relevant. The problem began with what you chose to say.

Make a note of this one. After all of the theoretical work is done, this is usually the single point of failure. For anyone who has tried paid ads, outreach, or content and only gotten unqualified leads, this is usually the cause.

Are you describing the problems of the customer you want?

Or are you writing about broad industry problems that are much more urgent to a beginner?

The answer affects your ads, content, lead magnets, emails, and sales page. One form field at the end cannot do all of the work of defining your customer.

We will go much deeper into this in Messaging. For now, understand that your research needs to stay at the right level.

### Experience With The Service Matters

Avoid doing done-for-you services for complete beginners. This is one of the most underrated pieces of advice I can give you.

I do not mean beginners in general. They can be experienced, successful, and running a large company. What matters is whether they have ever done the specific thing you sell.

A large company might have grown entirely through referrals, relationships, or events. That does not mean its team already knows how to respond to leads from paid advertising.

Someone might have built a $50 million business by speaking at events. Excellent business. It does not change the fact that they have never set an appointment, closed, or converted anybody who came from a paid ad. They have zero experience in it.

A successful founder may be an attractive buyer for a YouTube service. But if they have never filmed, part of the job may become helping them get comfortable on camera, develop ideas, and consistently record.

Those extra requirements affect your service even if they were not part of the original offer.

In my pay-per-lead agency, the single greatest indicator of whether a customer would succeed was whether they had bought leads before. It is over 95% correlated with whether they reach a third order.

Look at what that beat. Their revenue level. Their budget. The size of their company. How many sales reps they had. How much proof they had. How fast their speed to lead was. None of it came close to what one yes or no question told us.

If they had never bought leads before working with us, there was around a 5% chance they ever reached order number three.

You should look for the equivalent in your business.

What does the customer need to understand or already have in place to get a good result?

What happens when those things are missing?

That belongs in the ICP, because it affects whom you should sell the offer to in the first place.

### "Everyone Who Could Use It" Is Too Broad

If you sell auto insurance, your ICP is not "everyone with a car."

The ICP is:

- Age 25–60.
- Homeowners.
- Married, which implies more cars.
- Two or more cars.

More cars are more valuable, and home and auto insurance are often bundled.

Below age 25, a lot of the time they are either on their parents' insurance or less likely to have a car. Over 60, they are at the end of their driving career, and older people are in general less likely to convert.

The important point is the reason behind the segment. Household circumstances and the number of vehicles affect what is being purchased and what the customer is worth to the business.

The same thinking applies to your offer. Lots of people may have the general problem. Some will be much better customers for the particular way you solve it.

### Your Best Buyers And Your Best Customers May Differ

Someone can be easy to sell and difficult to serve.

Someone else might take longer to make a decision, then stay for years, get strong results, and be straightforward to work with.

If you refine your ICP only around who says yes fastest, you can build a customer base that looks good in the sales report and causes problems everywhere else.

Look at what happens throughout the relationship.

Which customers get good results?

Which stay?

Which are profitable after the actual work is done?

Which require constant exceptions to your process?

Then look for what those customers have in common.

You might begin with a looser definition of the customer and later find your strongest customers all have several sales reps, a call center, previous experience running ads, and operations across several markets.

That is useful information. It should change how you define your customer and how you market to them.

### Your ICP Develops As The Business Develops

The first version is based on what you know now.

You will refine it as you get more sales conversations, delivery experience, customer results, and proof.

That does not mean changing the customer every week. You need enough consistency to learn something. It means letting real experience improve the original decision.

As you get better, you may be able to serve customers who would have been beyond your ability at the beginning. As you learn, you may also stop selling to customers who repeatedly struggle to benefit from the offer.

Your offer, Big Idea, messaging, and proof should develop with that understanding. They all need to keep speaking to the customer the business is actually trying to serve.
