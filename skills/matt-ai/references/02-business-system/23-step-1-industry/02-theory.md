People spend an incredible amount of time trying to improve their offer without questioning the industry they are selling it to.

They change the promise. They change the price. They add another guarantee. They rebuild the funnel.

Sometimes the bigger opportunity is to take the skill they already have and apply it to a better market.

Your industry has an enormous effect on the potential of your business. It affects what a customer can pay you, how valuable your work is to them, how difficult they are to acquire, and how much of your delivery process you can use again.

That last part is what a lot of people miss.

We want to choose an industry where getting better at serving one customer helps us serve the next customer better too. If every new sale requires us to figure out a completely different business, getting more customers can mean getting more problems to solve personally.

### It Matters More What Boat You Are In Than How Hard You Row

That line is from one of Warren Buffett's letters to shareholders. He wrote about a classmate from university who was smarter than him, harder working and more focused. The classmate went on to have a very good career in the steel business. Buffett went into finance and became Warren Buffett.

There was nothing wrong with the steel business. It could not compare to where the other one ended up.

Buffett said this applies on a micro level to almost everybody, and it does. You can out-work the person in the better industry and still finish with the smaller business.

### The Same Service Can Be Worth More In A Different Industry

Imagine you run a marketing agency. You generate leads through paid ads, build the funnel, and handle appointment setting. Your client takes the sales calls and closes the deals.

Now imagine selling that service to wealth managers or dog walkers.

Which do you think will pay more and be a better client?

The basic work your agency performs could be very similar. You still need an ad, a page, a follow-up process, and someone to turn the enquiry into an appointment.

But the value of a new customer to those two businesses can be completely different.

A wealth manager might earn fees from a customer for years. A dog walker needs the price of acquiring a customer to make sense against what they can earn from walking that customer's dog.

If your service helps both businesses acquire ten customers, you have not necessarily created the same amount of value for both businesses. The difference in value changes what they can justify paying you.

Your labor does not automatically become ten times more expensive because the customer can earn ten times more from the result.

This is one reason the same skill set can produce very different businesses. You can become a better marketer, but you can also become more deliberate about whose problem you use that skill to solve.

When you evaluate an industry, look at the economics on the other side of the sale. What does your work allow that customer to do? What is that worth to them? What can they actually afford?

Those questions should come before another argument about the color of your booking button.

### What Happened When I Changed Industries

I sold my ecommerce agency and moved into insurance. Auto, home, life and Medicare.

Before that, I had never done more than $13 million of revenue in a year in an agency. The next year, doing insurance full time, I did $35 million, with a smaller team, less work, and pretty much the same skill set I already had.

Nothing about my ability changed in between. The market I pointed it at did.

I have watched other people do the same thing. Same offer, same process, same team, and the business doubles or triples. Sometimes far more than that, and sometimes almost immediately. It happens most often when someone leaves coaches, consultants and agency owners behind and moves into home services, insurance, healthcare or law.

### Changing Industries Is Easier For Some Businesses

If you have a skilled service business, you may be able to apply the same core skill to several industries.

Marketing, accounting, bookkeeping, and similar services can have this flexibility. There will still be industry knowledge to learn, but you may not need to rebuild the entire business from nothing.

Other businesses have much more tied to their existing market. A farmer cannot simply change what they grow overnight. A roofer cannot immediately become a kitchen remodeler. A physical location, equipment, qualifications, or inventory can make a change much harder.

So the point here is to understand the opportunity you are in. It is a decision to think through in the context of your actual business.

If you already have a successful business, you also have assets worth considering: customer relationships, proof, trained employees, and experience. Those have value. We want to understand whether the industry lets those assets compound, and whether a more specific customer within it would improve the situation.

### What I Mean By A Non-Industry

The most common problem I see is people serving what I call a non-industry.

These are labels such as:

- Ecommerce.
- SaaS.
- Agencies.
- Coaches.
- Consultants.
- Local businesses.
- Small businesses.

These labels tell you something about the customer. They often tell you very little about how you will solve the customer's problem.

Ecommerce tells you that a company sells online. SaaS tells you something about how software is sold. Local tells you where the business operates relative to its customers.

None of those labels, on their own, tells you that the businesses have the same buyers, the same economics, or a similar enough sales process for your work to transfer between them.

That is the distinction I am making with the term non-industry. We are looking for enough similarity to make the service repeatable.

If something feels wrong with your business and you cannot quite put your finger on what it is, this is usually it.

### Why Ecommerce Is A Useful Example

Suppose you run paid ads for ecommerce brands.

Your clients sell collagen, apparel, home goods, freeze-dried candy, protein powder, beef jerky, and beverages.

They all sell online. Several might use Shopify. You might manage their advertising through the same tools.

But the work that makes an ad successful can be different for every one of them.

The customer buying collagen may care about something completely different from the customer buying a shirt. The reasons someone trusts a supplement are different from the reasons they choose a beverage. The price, buying frequency, objections, and product demonstration can all change.

You find a winning ad for the collagen client. Great. How much of that success can you carry into the apparel account tomorrow?

You might learn something useful about the opening, the format, or how the product was shown. You probably cannot simply replace the collagen with a shirt and expect the same result.

Sometimes you cannot even take the same ad to another collagen brand and reproduce the result. Its price, positioning, customer base, and existing reputation may be different.

The issue is how much work has to be done again.

If every new client requires extensive research, new creative, a different explanation, and a different strategy, your growth creates a lot of new work. General experience still helps, but the service remains heavily dependent on people making fresh decisions for each account.

You can have a real business like this. I have built ecommerce agencies myself. I am explaining why this structure can make delivery harder to scale than it first appears.

### Your Industry Is Primarily For Fulfillment

People usually explain choosing a niche as a way to make marketing easier.

That part is useful. You can speak more specifically, build relevant proof, and become known for solving a particular problem.

But the main reason I want you to choose an industry is fulfillment.

At the beginning, getting customers feels like the entire business. You need someone to buy so you can get moving.

Once you have customers, you have to deliver. Then you need to deliver for more customers while keeping the quality high and the costs under control.

If all of that delivery depends on you personally figuring everything out, more sales do not remove that dependency. They make it more obvious.

We want an industry where your research, processes, assets, training, and technology become more useful as you serve more people.

That is what gives you a chance to build a delivery system that gets better over time.

If you are stuck somewhere in the $1 to $3 million per year range and you are serving a non-industry, this is your problem, and it is why the business never feels any easier month after month.

### What Can Carry Across Between Customers

Compare the ecommerce agency with an agency that generates leads for residential roofers.

The roofers still have differences. Their locations, competition, offers, reputations, and ability to handle enquiries matter. A campaign working in one city is not a guarantee that the numbers will be identical in another.

But a much larger part of the work can potentially carry across.

You have already researched common homeowner questions.

You know which concerns come up before someone books an inspection.

You have a qualification process, a follow-up sequence, reporting, and experience supporting the sale.

The next roofer gives you a place to apply and refine that work.

For example:

- Research: common homeowner problems, questions, and objections.
- Advertising: angles, formats, and explanations that have worked.
- Funnel: page structure, qualification questions, and follow-up.
- Sales support: scripts, response processes, and common objections.
- Measurement: tracking, reporting, and testing methods.

This is what I mean by aiming for copy-and-paste results. We want as much of the successful process as possible to be reusable, with the adjustments that customer actually needs.

You are starting from something you have already learned how to deliver.

When you discover a better follow-up message, several customers can benefit.

When you improve the onboarding process, your team can use it again.

When you build a better reporting system, it can improve the service across the business.

One improvement has somewhere else to go.

**How The Economics Can Change**

Here is a hypothetical comparison from the course. These are illustrative numbers, not industry averages.

Both agencies charge $5,000 per customer per month.

The heavily customized ecommerce service takes 70 delivery hours. Labor costs $3,500, and other direct fulfillment costs are $500.

$5,000 revenue − $4,000 direct fulfillment costs = $1,000 gross profit.

That is a 20% gross margin.

The more repeatable roofing service takes eight delivery hours. Labor costs $600, and other direct fulfillment costs are $200.

$5,000 revenue − $800 direct fulfillment costs = $4,200 gross profit.

That is an 84% gross margin.

The prices are identical. The amount left after delivering the service is very different.

That money still has to cover acquisition and the other expenses of running the business. But the second business has much more room to do that.

It also has more room to pay good people, invest in technology, and keep improving the service. In this example, it can pay a higher hourly labor rate and still have a much lower total delivery cost because it needs so many fewer hours.

This is why the number of hours required matters so much. Cheap labor is not the only way to make fulfillment less expensive.

### Retention Makes The Difference Larger

Now add another assumption to the example.

Suppose the ecommerce service has 20% monthly customer churn and the roofing service has 8%. Churn means the share of customers who leave during the period.

Using the simple estimate of one divided by monthly churn, that implies an average customer lifetime of five months in the first business and 12.5 months in the second. This is an illustration assuming a steady churn rate, not a forecast for an individual customer.

At $5,000 per month:

- Five months produces $25,000 in lifetime revenue.
- 12.5 months produces $62,500 in lifetime revenue.

Now apply the monthly gross profit from the example:

- $1,000 × five months = $5,000 in lifetime gross profit.
- $4,200 × 12.5 months = $52,500 in lifetime gross profit.

The second business earns more than ten times as much gross profit from the customer relationship under these assumptions.

Look at how much of the difference comes from the cost of doing the work and how long the customer stays. Both deserve attention when you choose who to serve.

A difference that looks manageable on one monthly invoice can become enormous over the whole relationship.

### The Compounding Sequence

Similar customers → reusable learning and processes → faster, more consistent delivery → better results and economics → more money and time to improve the business.

Better results also give you proof. That proof can help you sell, retain customers, build a reputation, and receive referrals.

Your team gets better training because the work is consistent enough to document. More routine work can be handled through an established process. Your strongest people can spend more of their time improving that process and handling the difficult cases.

You have less to figure out personally for every new customer.

Over time, you can afford stronger employees and a leadership team. Those people can make the business better again.

This is why choosing an industry is connected to your quality of life. The decision affects what happens to your workload when the business grows.

### Define The Industry Before You Score It

Repeatable fulfillment is the first thing I want you to understand. We also need to know whether the market can support the business you want to build.

Before you score anything, write down what you are actually scoring. Be exact.

You need five things written down: the industry, the location, the company size, the problem, and the expected price range.

Company size changes the customer's budget and buying process. Naming the exact problem keeps you scoring against a real customer instead of a vague idea of one. The price range keeps your Customer LTV and Spending Power scores grounded in a real number instead of a guess.

Your industry, your ICP and your offer have to be evaluated together (ICP and offer are both covered in upcoming courses). A market might look attractive in general but become much less attractive when you specify the location, company size, problem, and price.

### How We Evaluate An Industry

Making sure you are not in a non-industry is the first criteria. The framework is the second. The six scores below will not catch a non-industry for you, so settle that question before you start scoring.

There are six criteria in our framework. Each is worth five points, for a total of 30.

Score the average qualified customer you intend to serve. Finding one enormous business in the market does not mean the average customer has its budget or needs.

Use real data whenever possible. If exact data is unavailable, compare what you know with the descriptions and make your best estimate.

The examples in the framework show the signals behind each rating.

Only make that last one if your framework section has no dollar figures in it. If it does, leave the line exactly as it is.

### 1. High Customer LTV

How much gross profit is one average customer worth to a business in this industry over the whole relationship?

Going back to the marketing agency example, this means looking at our client's customer. How valuable is a homeowner to a roofing company? How valuable is a wealth-management client to a wealth manager?

That value affects what our client can afford to spend acquiring the customer, which affects what they can pay us.

There are a few ways to calculate it. Some people use revenue. Others use profit. I prefer gross-margin LTV, which is how much a customer is worth after paying the direct costs required to fulfill the product or service.

Suppose a company makes $5,000 per month from an average customer, that customer stays for 10 months, and the company has an 80% gross margin:

$5,000 × 10 × 0.80 = $40,000.

That customer has a gross-margin LTV of $40,000.

Look beyond the first transaction where repeat purchases are part of the business. Repairs, maintenance, and replacements can make a customer valuable over time.

Also distinguish revenue from gross profit. A large invoice does not mean the business keeps most of it.

Calculate one customer's LTV first, then look at the combined value of those customers.

A high customer value makes your service easier to justify. A lower value can still work if enough customers can be acquired and served profitably. What makes things difficult is low value combined with limited volume.

### 2. Easy To Reach

Can you identify qualified prospects and get your message in front of someone who can buy?

Think about paid ads, content, outreach, and partnerships. Ideally, you have more than one practical way to reach them.

Being able to produce a list of companies is useful, but it is only part of the problem. You still need access to the buyer. An assistant, purchasing process, or other gatekeeper can make that harder.

Some industries simply have more gatekeepers than others, and this is separate from the executive assistant you run into when you sell to larger companies. Doctors are the obvious case. You can export a list of them without any trouble, and then they are with patients all day and you are getting through their secretary before you get anywhere near them.

Easy to identify does not mean easy to reach.

At the strong end, you can identify suitable prospects, reach them through several channels, and speak to the decision-maker.

At the weak end, you are struggling to identify them at all and relying on vague signals to guess whether they might be relevant.

This matters because a valuable customer who is extremely expensive to reach may not support the acquisition costs your offer needs.

### 3. Large Market

How many qualified customers exist in the market you are actually targeting?

The relevant number includes your restrictions. Location, company size, capacity, and ability to pay all reduce the total.

Compare that with how many customers you need to build the business you want.

The required market size depends on your price, your customer LTV, your margins, your desired business size, your realistic market share, how frequently customers need the service, and how many customers you can fulfill for.

A business selling a $100 product needs a much larger market than a business selling a $100,000 service.

People often calculate their target market and then assume they can capture an unrealistic percentage of it. They say there are 10,000 potential customers and they only need 1,000.

That means you need 10% of the entire market. Depending on the industry, that could be extremely difficult or nearly impossible.

Some prospects will never hear about you. Some are too small. Some are too large. Some already have a solution. Some will never trust you. Some cannot afford you. Some are bad fits. Some will leave. Competitors will capture part of the market. The market may change over time.

Your real serviceable market is always smaller than the total number you initially find.

As a rough number, around 10,000 qualified people in your ICP is enough.

A smaller market can work if each customer is valuable enough. A huge market can still be unsuitable if almost nobody in it qualifies for your offer.

You do not need the entire market. You need enough reachable customers that the business can grow without requiring an unrealistic share of them.

It is usually much easier to build a large business by capturing 0.1% of something enormous than by attempting to capture 20% of something tiny.

A large market also gives you room to make mistakes. You can test different messages, target different segments, change pricing, move upmarket or downmarket, expand geographically, or introduce additional services.

A tiny market gives you very little room to maneuver. Once you burn through the most obvious prospects, growth becomes much harder.

This is another reason to evaluate your industry alongside your ICP (ICP is covered in the next course). "All ecommerce stores" is a very different market from established stores in a particular country that can afford your service.

### 4. Significant Pain

How painful is the problem you intend to solve, and what happens if the customer does nothing?

The problem should ideally be expensive, frequent, urgent, frustrating, easy to recognize, and difficult to ignore.

The greater the pain, the less persuasion is required. If the customer is losing $100,000 per month because of a problem, they will behave very differently from someone who is mildly annoyed by it.

Some problems interrupt the business or create an immediate loss. Others are improvements the owner would like to make eventually.

The customer might agree that your service is useful while feeling no urgency to buy it.

A roofing company with available crews and too few jobs has a different situation from one that is fully booked and struggling to fulfill its existing work. The same lead-generation offer can be urgent to one and badly timed for the other.

This does not mean you should manufacture pain or exaggerate it. It means you should select a market that already experiences a serious problem your offer can genuinely solve.

Score the problem your offer actually solves for the customer you actually want. A serious problem elsewhere in the industry does not automatically make your service important.

### 5. Spending Power

Can the average qualified customer afford what you intend to charge?

This is different from customer lifetime value.

A new customer might be worth a lot to them eventually, but the business still needs money available to pay for your service now.

Spending power does not simply mean the customer technically has the money. It means they can pay your price without the purchase putting their entire business at risk. The purchase should make sense in the context of their revenue, margins, cash flow, customer LTV, and existing spending.

If you charge $10,000 per month, selling to businesses making $20,000 per month will probably create constant problems. Even if you convince them to buy, the relationship will be under pressure from the first day.

Every delay will feel catastrophic.

Every small performance fluctuation will become an emergency.

Every invoice will become a discussion.

They need an immediate miracle because they cannot comfortably afford to wait.

Compare that with a company making $5 million per year with strong margins and an existing marketing budget. A $10,000 monthly service still needs to create value, but it is not threatening the survival of the company. The client can think rationally. They can tolerate testing. They can give the service enough time to work.

The useful question is how long the average qualified customer could comfortably pay without needing an immediate result.

Someone can desperately need what you sell and still be unable to buy it. That is a difficult customer to build your economics around.

There is a major difference between needing the service, wanting the service, and being able to pay for the service. A good market has all three.

You should not feel guilty about targeting businesses with spending power. A customer who can comfortably afford your offer is usually easier to serve, more patient, more rational, and more capable of implementing what you recommend.

The strongest situation is a customer who can comfortably afford the service and can see why it is worth buying. At the weak end, the purchase creates financial strain before the work even starts.

This is one of the reasons we will spend so much time on the level of customer we choose in the ICP section.

### 6. Growing Market

Is the industry growing, remaining stable, or declining?

A growing market can give you more opportunities as time passes. A shrinking market means you may have to win an increasing share just to maintain the same business.

A stable market is completely fine. You do not need a fashionable industry to build a valuable company.

Use market data from the previous three years where possible. Look at what is happening to demand, customer spending, and the number of people buying.

A fast-growing market gives you tailwinds. A dying market means you are fighting the current.

**Add The Six Scores Together**

Six criteria, five points each, for a total out of 30.

- 27 to 30: Excellent
- 24 to 26: Good
- 20 to 23: Neutral
- 15 to 19: Poor
- 6 to 14: Bad

Five-star scores should be rare.

Anything less than neutral and I would definitely consider changing industries. If it comes out bad, I would plan to quit everything I am doing right now and start brainstorming my next opportunity.

### The Total Is Not The Answer

Look at the individual scores as well as the total.

A large market does not fix customers who cannot afford you. Easy access does not fix a problem nobody cares about.

A score of one or two for Customer LTV, Easy to Reach, Significant Pain, or Spending Power can make the industry extremely difficult regardless of how well it scores everywhere else.

None of these attributes should be viewed entirely on its own. The ideal market is easy to reach, has spending power, experiences serious pain, and is large enough.

A large market with no pain will not buy. A painful market with no spending power cannot pay. A wealthy market that is impossible to reach will be expensive to acquire. An easy-to-reach market that is extremely small will place a ceiling on the business.

You are looking for the combination.

Imagine two options. The first has severe pain but very little money, there are millions of them, and they are easy to reach. The second has severe pain, significant spending power, high customer LTV, and there are 50,000 of them you can reliably identify.

The second market is smaller and probably much more economically attractive.

You are not trying to choose the market containing the people who need the most help.
