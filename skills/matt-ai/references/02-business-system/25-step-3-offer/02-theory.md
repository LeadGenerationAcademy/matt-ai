For this course, your offer is the thing you sell to cold traffic to get someone in the door.

Cold traffic means people who do not already know you, trust you, or have a relationship with your business. They see an ad, visit a page, watch a video sales letter, or receive an outreach message. The offer is what you are asking them to buy.

You may have a larger recurring service you sell after that. We are focusing on the front-end offer because this is what gives a new customer a reason to begin the relationship.

If you can sell that offer profitably to people who do not already know you, you have something powerful. You can deliberately work on acquisition instead of depending on referrals, relationships, or someone watching your content for months.

But the offer has to work in the context of your industry, ICP, economics, Big Idea, messaging, and proof. Writing a bigger promise does not fix all of those things.

### Your Offer Is Only Good Or Bad In Relation To Your ICP

There is no universally great offer.

A $50,000 service can sound ridiculously expensive to one customer and perfectly reasonable to another. The price means something different depending on the result, the customer's resources, and what solving the problem is worth to them.

The same is true of the promise. Get your first agency client may be very desirable to someone who has never made a sale. It means almost nothing to an agency owner already doing $5 million per year.

This is why we defined the ICP first. The offer is built for that customer's problem at that customer's level. You cannot judge it by asking whether the words sound exciting to you.

A multi-state roofing company with a call center, dozens of sales representatives and dozens of crews is not the same business as a solo owner-operator who is essentially self-employed. Both are technically roofing companies. They have entirely different problems, goals, resources and levels of sophistication.

They should not respond to the same messaging.

### Three Things Need To Happen Before Someone Buys

There are three things we need to establish:

1. They understand what the offer is.
2. They believe it can solve their problem.
3. They believe you are the person or business to deliver it.

These are different jobs. Someone can understand what you sell without believing they need it. They can believe the service is useful without trusting you to perform it. They can like you and still have no idea what they would receive after paying.

This is why adding more proof does not solve every sales problem. If the customer cannot understand the offer, another testimonial will not resolve the confusion. And a clear explanation is sometimes not enough either. They know what it is. They still need a reason to believe that approach will work for them.

They literally will not and literally cannot buy unless all three are true. This holds in every industry, with every business type, for every offer that has ever existed.

### Sell Something People Already Feel They Must Buy

One of the best ways to make offer creation easier is to choose a problem people already feel they must address. You spend less time creating interest in the category. The buyer already recognizes the need and may already understand the type of solution.

There are three broad versions of this: legally required, functionally required, and competitively required.

The point is to understand how much of the buying decision already exists before the customer encounters you.

### Legally Required

Some needs come from a legal obligation. Required insurance coverage, tax obligations and compliance requirements are examples. The buyer has an external reason to deal with the problem.

They are less likely to need a long explanation of why the category matters. They may already be actively looking for someone to help.

That does not mean they must buy from you, or that they must outsource something they could handle themselves. You still have to explain why your service is the right choice. But the existence of the problem is much less open to debate.

Auto insurance is the clearest version of this. If you spent your entire career in that industry, there is not a single time you would have to introduce somebody to the concept of auto insurance. They already know what it is, and they already believe it is the solution, because it is the thing itself. Two of the three conditions are handled before you say a word. All you have left is convincing them yours is better than the alternative.

### Functionally Required

Other needs come from how life or the business works. Food and shelter are obvious examples. There may be several ways to obtain them, but the need does not disappear because someone ignores an ad.

A related example is a purchase required by another party before a transaction can proceed, such as coverage a lender requires as part of financing.

The buyer has a practical reason to act. They need to solve the problem to continue doing something else they care about. Your service can still have competitors, but you are selling into existing demand.

### Competitively Required

Then there are things a business needs to do if it wants to perform well and grow. Lead generation and ecommerce email marketing are good examples. A business can handle these internally or through another provider. It still needs the work done well if it wants to grow.

That gives the offer a connection to something already important.

This category still leaves you with more selling to do than a clearly required purchase. The customer might agree that lead generation matters and disagree that your method is the right way to do it.

### Understanding The Category Is Different From Choosing Your Method

Most established business owners understand the concept of generating leads. That does not mean they believe they should buy your pay-per-lead service, hire your paid ads agency, join your program, or use your outreach system.

They might prefer content, referrals, networking, partnerships, or doing the work internally. They might want a project instead of a retainer, or done-for-you instead of training. All of these are different answers to the same broad need.

So your offer may have the first buying condition handled. You still have to explain why your approach fits their situation and why they should trust you to deliver it.

### The Value Needs To Be Obvious

In a business-to-business setting, most offers create value through some combination of:

- Making more money.
- Saving time.
- Reducing costs.
- Reducing risk.
- Increasing speed.

One valuable result can be enough. You do not need the offer to do everything on the list.

Making more money is always the most powerful because it is the easiest to justify. You can do the math on paper.

The other benefits can be valuable while remaining abstract. Save four hours per day sounds useful. But what does that mean over a year? Who saves those hours? What is the purchase worth relative to its price?

Do that thinking with the customer. Do not rely on them doing the math in their head. Write it out for them. Make a chart if it helps.

### Turning Time Saved Into A Financial Example

Suppose your offer saves the customer four hours per day and costs $15,000.

4 hours per day × 5 days per week × 50 weeks = 1,000 hours saved per year.

At a $15,000 price, those 1,000 hours only need to be worth $15 each to break even.

- $30 per hour: $30,000 per year, 2.0x
- $50 per hour: $50,000 per year, 3.3x
- $75 per hour: $75,000 per year, 5.0x
- $100 per hour: $100,000 per year, 6.7x
- $125 per hour: $125,000 per year, 8.3x

If the person's time is worth $75 per hour, saving four hours per day creates roughly $75,000 of annual value from a $15,000 purchase. That is a 5x multiple.

### Turning Risk Reduction Into A Financial Example

The same principle applies to risk.

Say you have a compliance offer. You sell to healthcare professionals who need to securely store patient data. There are laws around this, and if they get hacked, they can face pretty hefty fines.

For this example, the average fine is $250,000 and your compliance service costs $2,500 per year.

I would literally spell this out for them.

"If you have a data breach once every hundred years, this will pay for itself."

That is how you translate reducing risk into a pure dollar ROI.

### Your Unique Mechanism Should Make Sense

A unique mechanism is the believable explanation for why the offer should produce the result. Its job is to make someone read the page and think, "That makes sense." Nothing more. It does not need to be fancy, and usually should not be.

There are two questions behind it. Why would this work when they have tried what is, in theory, the same thing before and it failed? And how does it work in simple terms?

Calling your service the Six-Step Email Revenue Vortex does not explain how anything works. You have given the customer another phrase to decode. A weird name that makes people wince is not a unique mechanism, and it does not impress anyone.

If you are trying to grow, you will be selling to people who actually know what they are talking about. Give an ordinary process a stupid name and you will probably embarrass yourself in front of them.

The worst part is you will never know. They will not message you to explain that your Revenue Vortex System sounds ridiculous. They will simply move on with their lives, and you will be none the wiser.

### Guarantees Have A Specific Job

People put far too much weight on making the guarantee bigger. Guarantees have become so played out and increasingly unrealistic that they have kind of killed themselves for the entire market. I blame Alex Hormozi for that.

A guarantee can help someone feel comfortable enough to click your ad and book a call. That is a perfectly good use for it.

There is a second one. Having real money at risk creates a very strong incentive to make sure your fulfillment works. If you do not deliver, you are going to have a lot of refunds.

Beyond those two, do not expect it to do anything else. Do not expect it to improve your show rate. Do not expect it to improve your close rate. Do not expect it to compensate for a weak offer or bad marketing. That is the job of the offer and the job of your marketing.

The fucked up part is that a huge percentage of people get on the call and do not even remember what the guarantee was, or whether there was one at all.

There is a cost to them as well. Guarantees are more likely to attract the kind of client you do not want. And if someone is very obsessed with your guarantee during the sales process, and is only buying because of it, that is a huge red flag.

Over time, you want enough proof and reputation that you do not need some insane guarantee to get people to buy. The guarantee is the cherry on top, not the reason they believe you.

We also need to be able to honor what we promise. A guarantee is part of the actual offer, with consequences for delivery and economics.

### Do Not Guarantee A Huge Range

"We will get you 5-50 booked calls per month" is a very unclear promise. The customer could be receiving ten times as much at one end of the range as at the other.

I have also seen a guarantee ranging from five million to 500 million views. That makes the same problem even more obvious.

Guarantee something specific or not at all.

### "We Will Work For Free Until We Do"

I do not like this as a substitute for meaningful risk reversal.

If the customer has already spent money and waited for a result that did not arrive, more time with the same provider does not solve the problem they now have. They may have lost an opportunity. They may have lost confidence in the work. The original delay may matter more than another month of fees.

Think about the guarantee from their position. What happens when they invoke it? Does that outcome actually reduce the downside they care about?

### Do Not Promise Results You Cannot Control

For a lead-generation service, the customer often controls the final sale. You can generate enquiries, build the funnel, send reminders and support appointment setting. If their team handles the sales calls, you do not control whether those calls become customers.

In many cases the strongest offer combines a clear deliverable with an intended result.

"We will analyze your complete email account, identify the five biggest revenue leaks, rebuild your seven highest-value flows, and launch the first three campaigns within 30 days, with the goal of increasing the percentage of revenue generated through email."

The customer understands exactly what they receive, and the result the work is designed to create. That gives them certainty without forcing you to guarantee something you cannot completely control.

The same deliverable can produce very different outcomes depending on the customer's ability to use it.

### The Offer Also Has To Be Good For Your Business

You can make an offer more attractive by promising more, charging less, doing more work and accepting more risk. Keep doing that without considering your own business and you end up selling something you regret having to deliver.

What is lost on a lot of people, especially your typical direct-response gurus, is that the offer is not just about making grander and grander claims. It actually has to be good for your business too.

### Acquisition And Fulfillment Have To Be Paid For

The offer needs to cover the cost of getting the customer and delivering what you sold. Our target is eventually to collect at least 2X those combined costs upfront. The Unit Economics lesson explains this in detail.

If acquisition costs $2,000 and fulfillment costs $1,000, the combined cost is $3,000. The target is at least $6,000 collected from the sale.

That is cash actually received. A large contract paid in small installments does not give you the full contract value to spend immediately.

Most people are never limited by their ads or their funnel or their sales team. They are limited by their unit economics.

### The Offer Should Become Easier To Deliver

We want a standardized front-end offer with a process you can improve. Every time you fulfill it, your processes, proof, speed, margins and results should improve.

If every sale produces an entirely new project with little in common with the last one, fewer of those improvements transfer.

For the front-end offer, a repeatable service gives you a much clearer path to lower costs and more capacity. Choosing similar customers makes it easier to standardize the work.

### The Problem Should Still Matter In Five Years

An offer built around a lasting problem can survive changes in tactics and tools. A business will still need customers. It will still need to control costs, complete work and deal with risk. The software or advertising method may change.

Avoid building your entire offer around a temporary tactic, trend or platform feature that forces you to start over constantly.

You rarely see people get insane results with something until around year three. Go evaluate anyone you admire and look at when they started their current offer. I would bet it was at least three years ago.

Compounding takes a lot of time. When you constantly start over, you never get the benefit of it. Your offer does not have to work forever. It should last long enough for the time, money and effort you put into building it to be worth it.

### The Next Offer Should Make Sense

If you have a larger recurring service, the front-end offer should create a sensible beginning to that relationship. It should solve a useful immediate problem and make the next step understandable.

You do not need to force every business into a complicated ladder of offers. If the initial offer is already your main service, this check may not apply.

The first purchase still needs to be valuable on its own. It should not exist only as an excuse to sell something else.

### Evaluate The Offer From The Customer's Side

Part A of the framework asks eight questions:

1. Does it make sense to cold traffic?
2. How strongly does the customer want or need the result?
3. Is the value obvious relative to the price?
4. Do they believe it can work for them?
5. Is the result or deliverable clearly defined?
6. How quickly do they receive their first meaningful value?
7. How much effort do they need to contribute?
8. How much downside do they face if it does not work?

Each is scored one to five, for a total of 40. The ranges are 36-40 exceptional, 32-35 strong, 24-31 acceptable with weaknesses, 16-23 weak, 8-15 terrible.

The point is to find the individual weakness. A strong total should not distract you from a part of the offer that is clearly causing problems.

Also remember that the customer's fee is only part of the risk. They can lose time, opportunities, reputation, and money spent implementing the service.

### Part A Is The Customer, Part B Is You

Part A is the point of view of the customer. Part B is the point of view of your business. Both matter, and both should be evaluated separately.

Part B is the part most people never get to. It covers whether the offer pays for the cost of acquiring and serving the customer, whether you can fulfill it consistently without rebuilding it every time, whether it will still be relevant in five years, and whether completing it creates the need for your main recurring service.

Even if your total is high, improve every individual category scoring below four stars. Start with your lowest, improve it, and complete the audit again.

### The Value Formula

Once the offer addresses a worthwhile problem and you have scored it honestly, we can use Alex Hormozi's value formula to make it more attractive.

Value = (Desired Result × Perceived Likelihood Of Achievement) ÷ (Time Delay × Effort And Sacrifice).

This is a thinking model, not a literal formula that calculates a price.

There are four ways to make an offer more valuable. Give the customer a better result. Make them believe they are more likely to achieve it. Give them value faster. Require less work from them.

People focus almost entirely on the size of the result. The other three can make an enormous difference without requiring a bigger promise.

### Desired Result

How badly does this specific customer want the result?

Go back to the ICP work. For an established agency owner, useful outcomes might include building a second acquisition channel, getting out of sales, increasing ad spend without acquisition costs becoming unworkable, or having a marketing team that generates customers without the owner doing everything.

You could describe all of those loosely as growing the business. The more specific versions help the customer see what would change.

Your offer needs one clear result that is important enough to justify the purchase.

### Perceived Likelihood Of Achievement

How likely does the customer believe it is that they will get the result from you?

A huge promise has very little value to someone who thinks it will never happen.

You increase belief through relevant proof, case studies, clear deliverables, a believable explanation of how the offer works, and answers to their major objections.

The customer needs to believe more than "this has worked for someone." They need to see why it could work for them. This is one of the biggest marketing benefits of working in a specific industry. More of the results you earn are relevant to the next prospect.

### Time Delay

How long does the customer wait after paying before receiving something meaningfully valuable?

This is not necessarily the time required to achieve the complete result. A service might take six months to finish while providing a useful first result within days. That first result matters, and the customer needs to know about it before buying.

A good example is the content offer from earlier, the one guaranteeing five million to 500 million views over an entire year. The business actually published the customer's first content within 48 hours, but barely communicated that. The offer sounded much slower than it was.

Improve this by simplifying onboarding, standardizing fulfillment, and giving the customer several quick wins immediately. In a lead-generation service, that might mean helping with a sales script before the first leads arrive.

Those improvements need to be useful in their own right. A welcome email is not automatically meaningful value.

### Why Faster Can Be Worth More

The weight loss industry shows this better than anything.

Working out and eating right on your own takes a long time, which is why calorie tracking apps can only charge around $9.99 a month.

A personal trainer and a meal plan get there faster, so they charge more.

Liposuction works immediately, which is why surgeons charge thousands or tens of thousands.

The outcome barely changes as you move up that list. The speed changes, and the price follows it.

Your actual offer has real limits. Some outcomes take time. We are looking for the parts of the wait that exist because of an inefficient process rather than because the result requires them.

If the customer waits a week because nobody sent the access request, that is something you can fix.

### Effort And Sacrifice

How much does the customer have to do to get the result?

Count onboarding, meetings, homework, decisions, approvals, learning software, recording material, managing people and doing the implementation. Those requirements are part of the offer even when they are not on the sales page.

Imagine two offers with the same result, price, speed and likelihood of success.

Offer A: three calls per week, homework, you learn the process yourself, you build everything yourself, and another five hours per week implementing it.

Offer B: a 30-minute onboarding call, you give them access, and they do basically everything else for you.

Which one would you rather buy? Obviously Offer B.

This is the primary reason done-for-you commands a higher price than done-with-you or do-it-yourself. You are absorbing the effort and sacrifice, and the price moves with it.

This is also why you should constantly look through your fulfillment and ask, why the fuck are we making the customer do this. If they do not need to do it, remove it.

Removing unnecessary customer work improves the offer without changing the result or cutting the price.

### The Work You Cannot Remove

Some services require ongoing customer participation. A YouTube service may handle research, ideas, editing and publishing, while the customer still has to film. A lead-generation service delivers enquiries, and their team still has to sell.

They also put a ceiling on the business. Content agencies generally do not reach the scale that paid ads or outreach agencies reach, and this is exactly why. The customer still has to get in front of the camera, and you can never take that off them.

Those requirements should influence your ICP, onboarding and sales explanation. A customer who cannot or will not do their part may be a poor fit regardless of how much they can afford.

Being clear about this before the sale is better than discovering it during delivery.

### Improve Your Lowest Score First, Then Score It Again

Find your lowest of the four and fix that one before you touch anything else.

To improve Dream Outcome, offer a result that makes them more money, saves more time, reduces more risk, or solves a more important problem. To improve Perceived Likelihood, add better proof, relevant case studies, clearer deliverables and a believable mechanism. To improve Time to First Value, simplify onboarding and give quick wins immediately. To improve Effort and Sacrifice, reduce meetings, decisions, information requests and required implementation.

Then score all four again, not just the one you improved. Repeat until every category scores at least four stars.

The dream is the biggest result, with the highest likelihood of success, as quickly as possible, while requiring as little effort from them as possible.

### Be Careful With Customers Who Are Beginners At Your Service

A successful business is not automatically an experienced buyer of what you sell.

Someone who has never bought leads may need to build a response and sales process. Someone who has never run ads may be used to closing warm referrals. Someone starting YouTube may need help becoming comfortable on camera before your production process can work.

In my pay-per-lead agency, the greatest indicator of whether a customer would succeed was whether they had bought leads before.

A lot of people with paid ad agencies or YouTube agencies think it is a good idea to help a successful business owner get started with paid ads or on YouTube. Great opportunity, right? Spoiler alert. It is not.

If you choose to serve beginners, build the required education and support into the actual offer. Do not sell a service designed for an experienced customer and hope the missing pieces sort themselves out.

### Perception Is What The Customer Has Before Buying

You might believe your service is excellent. The prospect has not experienced it yet.

You hear it all the time. "Our service is the best." It probably is not, but even if it is, nobody knows that until after they buy. Before that, they are going off their perception of what they can see.

It does not matter if we nail their desired result, have the most proof ever, get them extremely fast value, and they barely have to do a thing, if we do not clearly say it before they even get on a call with us.

If you deliver value within 48 hours but your marketing sounds like a year-long wait, the buyer evaluates the longer wait. If most of the work is done for them but the page suggests a complicated implementation, they evaluate that instead.

Check whether the perception of your offer matches the reality of it in:

- Your ad
- Your sales page above the fold
- Your VSL
- The rest of your sales page
- Your email flows
- Your sales process
- Your proof

A lot of the time it will not match, and you will be operating at 10% of the potential that you could be.

### The Objections You Never Hear

The objections you hear on sales calls are not the worst or biggest ones. They are a very small percentage of the total. Those people still booked.

The deadliest objections are the ones that stop someone from booking in the first place. They saw your ad, your VSL or your sales page and thought it was too expensive, it probably would not work for them, it sounded like too much work, it would take forever, or they did not trust it.

Then they leave. You never get the chance to handle the objection.

That is why we identify and answer objections before the sales call, through our ads, VSL, sales page, emails, proof and content. The goal is to eliminate them before they ever become objections.

There are usually six to 12 core objections that come up repeatedly. At a minimum, every one should have a case study showing how you overcame that problem for someone, an email explaining how you overcame it for someone, and a specific point in your sales presentation covering it.

Then use stories, metaphors and how-to content to address the same objection through your ads and content. Some people resonate with the story. Others understand the metaphor. Others respond to the direct how-to.

Six objections with three ways to address each gives you 18 ways to communicate around them. Twelve gives you 36.

By the time they speak with you, you want them to feel as though you have already answered the questions they were preparing to ask.

### Everything You Build Next Is The Same Offer

The offer is now audited and amplified. The rest of this course does not change it. What it does is put the same offer into the formats you will actually use.

Every one carries the same ICP, main problem, current circumstances, desired outcome, mechanism, deliverables, timeline, proof, price, risk reversal and next step. Change the mechanism between two of them and you have built two different offers.

Everything you scored in the audit and the amplification only exists to a prospect if it appears in what they can actually see.

### The Presentation Is What You Use On The Call

Thirteen slides whose only job is to explain the offer, answer the major objections, and ask them to buy. One clear idea per slide.

Your proof has to match the level of customer you want. A case study built to resonate with a smaller, less sophisticated company proves nothing to a much larger one with completely different problems.

### The Sales Letter Is The Written Version

The same offer, read without you in the room. If you could not hear yourself saying it out loud, rewrite it.

Keep the paragraphs short and make sure someone skimming only the headlines still understands what you are selling.

### Thirty Seconds, Then Seven

Two spoken versions for when someone asks what you do. The 30-second pitch should make them want to ask the next question. The one-liner makes two things clear, who you help and what result you provide.

Both get timed, not guessed at. Practice until there is no hesitation, no umms, and no uhhs.

### The One-Pager Is What You Send

Everything someone needs to understand the offer, on one page, for the person who wants more information but not your whole sales letter.

It is literal. If it does not fit inside one screenshot on your phone, it is not a one-pager.

### The Loom Turns The One-Pager Into A Conversation

Three minutes of you talking through the one-pager and showing proof. Go over your last ten clients and step by step show the results they got. Include some bad ones too, to make it more believable.

Do not leave it waiting for someone to ask. Send it before the call, not only when someone requests it.
