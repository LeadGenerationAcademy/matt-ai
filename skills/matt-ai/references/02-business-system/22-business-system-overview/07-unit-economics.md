Unit economics is the math behind your offer: what you collect, what it costs to acquire a customer, what it costs to fulfill, and when the money comes in.

Everyone understands that an offer needs to be profitable. But an offer can be profitable on paper while being cash flow negative.

If you spend $5,000 today to acquire and fulfill for a customer, and they pay you $1,000 per month for ten months, you ultimately collect $10,000. But after the first payment, you are still $4,000 in the hole.

Now imagine acquiring 100 customers that way. You need an enormous pile of cash just to fund your growth.

That is why we care about the cash collected on the first sale. A $30,000 contract paid in twelve installments of $2,500 gives you $2,500 in initial cash collected.

Our target is to collect at least 2x the combined acquisition and fulfillment costs up front.

If acquiring and fulfilling for a customer costs $5,000, we want to collect at least $10,000. That covers the first customer and leaves another $5,000 to fund the next one.

Most people will work toward this in stages. First, get someone to buy. Then work toward breaking even on the initial cash collected. Improve fulfillment, improve acquisition, and raise prices as proof and demand increase.

Once the economics work, fulfillment capacity becomes the next constraint. This is why standardization matters so much. We want to build our people, processes, training, and technology around delivering the same thing repeatedly.
