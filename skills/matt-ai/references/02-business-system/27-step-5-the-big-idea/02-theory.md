A Big Idea is the core concept that everything in your marketing comes back to.

It gives your ads, content, sales conversations, and proof something consistent to reinforce. You can talk about different problems without making the business sound like it believes something different every week.

For a long time, this was the missing piece for me. I had an internal version of this course for years, and I understood the importance of industry, ICP, offers, and economics. The Big Idea helped connect all of that work to the marketing people actually see.

Without one, you can have individually useful ads and videos that never add up to much in the customer's mind.

They see something about leads, then something about sales, then something about hiring. Each piece might be good. But they still struggle to explain what you stand for or why they should see your offer as the answer.

A strong Big Idea gives those separate pieces a shared point.

### Your Big Idea Makes The Offer Make Sense

The Big Idea should help the customer understand why the offer is relevant and desirable.

It connects something they care about to the reason your solution makes sense.

That is a different job from listing deliverables. Your offer explains what they buy. Your mechanism explains how the service works. Your Big Idea gives the wider belief that makes the solution worth considering.

You can see this in a business built around helping established owners reach a particular scale. It might teach advertising, sales, hiring, and systems. Those are different subjects, but they can all support the same belief about what is possible for that business and what it needs to get there.

The customer understands why those pieces belong together.

The same applies to a much more specific service. If your ICP has spare delivery capacity and needs more demand, your marketing can repeatedly demonstrate the connection between qualified lead flow and business growth.

Different topics become different ways of making the same idea understandable.

### You Can Say The Same Thing In Many Ways

A Big Idea does not mean repeating the same ad forever.

You can use a customer story, explain a process, answer an objection, demonstrate a result, or discuss a problem the buyer is experiencing.

Each piece can introduce something new while supporting the same central idea.

This makes marketing easier to build on. You are not starting from an empty page and a completely new position every time you need a video.

It also helps the audience remember you. If they repeatedly see useful material connected to the same subject, you have a better chance of becoming associated with it.

That association is earned through what you consistently show and explain. One line in a brand document does not create it.

### Start With Something Your ICP Already Believes

Ideally, your Big Idea begins with something the customer already recognizes as true or important.

That might be a belief about their business, a desire they already have, a frustration they experience, or an ambition connected to their identity.

This is similar to why I like offers that address things people already feel they must buy. Less of the marketing has to be spent creating interest from nothing.

Changing someone's established beliefs can be difficult, especially in the short time you have their attention. It is usually easier to begin with something they recognize and show a useful connection they have not fully considered.

You might start with a business owner's existing belief that unused capacity costs them money. The marketing then explains how lead flow affects their ability to keep that capacity productive.

The starting point belongs to the customer. The connection helps them understand your approach.

### Familiar Enough To Believe, Different Enough To Notice

There is a balance here.

If your idea only repeats something obvious in the same way everyone else says it, people may have little reason to remember you.

If it requires them to reject everything they know and accept an unsupported claim, they may not believe it.

The useful middle is often a familiar belief with a different implication.

You help them see an existing problem more clearly, recognize an opportunity, or understand why a different approach could work.

This is why building on an existing belief and presenting a distinct perspective are compatible. The whole idea does not need to be something they have heard before. It needs a believable starting point and a connection you can explain.

Your proof and stories do much of the work of making that connection credible.

### A Memorable Idea Still Has To Be True

An idea can be simple, emotional, and easy to repeat while overstating what the evidence supports.

Our framework evaluates how well an idea communicates. It does not turn the claim into a fact.

This matters because exaggeration can make an idea sound stronger on the surface and weaker as soon as a thoughtful customer examines it.

If the entire idea falls apart when someone names an obvious exception, you have work to do.

We want something we can keep explaining and demonstrating over time. The business should be able to deliver in a way that supports the belief.

### The Ten Checks

There are ten checks in the Big Idea framework. Each is scored from one to five.

They help us look at clarity, relevance, credibility, and how well the idea connects the rest of the business.

You do not need to invent a complicated concept to score well. A simple idea can be very powerful when it fits the customer and is consistently supported.

### 1. Simple And Repeatable

Can someone understand the idea and repeat it accurately in one sentence?

If the idea contains several competing messages, people may remember only a fragment or interpret it differently from how you intended.

You need one identifiable core concept.

This does not mean your entire service can be explained in one sentence. The Big Idea gives someone a clear thing to remember while the rest of your marketing develops the detail.

At the strongest level, someone hears it once and can explain it to another person. At the weakest level, there is no clear idea to repeat at all.

### 2. Relevant To The Exact ICP

Does the idea connect to something this specific customer wants, fears, believes, or experiences?

Relevance to the industry is not enough.

An ambitious established owner may care about building a much larger company. A beginner may care about getting the first customer. A message about running a sales team will mean different things to those two people.

This is why the ICP work comes first. We need to know what the person already cares about before deciding what our marketing should repeatedly reinforce.

The best ideas feel connected to the customer's actual situation. Weak ones sound as though they could belong to any business selling anything.

### 3. Unexpected And Distinct

Does it give the customer a different way to think about the problem or a reason to reconsider what is possible?

Being unusual by itself is not useful. The difference needs to matter to the buyer.

You might identify a neglected cause of their problem or connect a familiar problem to a better approach. You might make a worthwhile opportunity feel more concrete and achievable.

The claim still needs to be believable.

A strong perspective can get attention without making the customer feel that you are being extreme merely to be noticed.

### 4. Concrete

Can the person picture what the idea means?

Specific people, situations, outcomes, numbers, and examples make this easier.

A target such as $1 million per month is concrete because someone can understand what is being discussed. A story about keeping an additional crew busy is concrete because the business owner can imagine the effect on the company.

Vague claims about transformation, excellence, and success leave the customer to supply the meaning.

The more clearly they can picture the idea, the easier it becomes to understand and discuss.

### 5. Credible

What reason does the customer have to believe it?

Relevant results, demonstrations, specific examples, firsthand experience, and a sensible mechanism can all help.

The evidence should support the claim you actually make. Showing that more leads can create more jobs supports a connection between lead flow and growth. It does not prove that lead flow is the only thing a business ever needs.

A stronger claim creates a greater burden of proof.

If you have a promising idea but limited evidence, recognize that the proof still needs to be built. A high opinion of your wording is not a substitute for it.

### 6. Emotional

Does the idea give the customer a reason to care personally?

Money, time, security, freedom, identity, status, and ambition can all matter here.

For an owner, keeping crews busy is more than an operational statistic. They have built a company, hired people, and taken on obligations. A lack of work affects how they feel about the business and where it is going.

The emotion should come from the customer's real situation. You do not need to add dramatic language to something they already care deeply about.

A logical idea can still work. The question is whether you understand the personal importance behind it.

### 7. Demonstrated Through Stories

Can people see the idea happening in a real situation?

Founder experiences, customer transformations, and specific before-and-after examples make the concept easier to follow.

A story gives the person context. They can understand the starting problem, what changed, what was done, and the result.

One strong story can be useful. More relevant stories show the idea across different situations and give the audience more opportunities to recognize themselves.

If you are discussing a hypothetical example, label it that way. It can explain the idea, but it does not prove that you have already delivered the result.

### 8. Makes The Offer Feel Logical

After someone understands the Big Idea, does the offer make sense as a way to act on it?

An idea can attract attention and still have a weak connection to what you sell.

If you teach that a certain opportunity is valuable, the offer should help the customer access it. If you explain why a particular problem limits their growth, the offer should address that problem.

The customer should be able to follow the connection without you forcing it.

This is what keeps the Big Idea useful to the business. We want an idea that supports the buying decision, not simply a memorable opinion.

### 9. Reinforced Across The Business

Do the ads, content, sales page, sales process, proof, and offer support the same belief?

They can cover different subjects. Consistency does not require identical wording.

But someone should not encounter a different explanation of what matters at every stage.

If the marketing says speed is central, the delivery should support that. If the idea is about dependable lead flow, your content and proof should help people understand and evaluate that claim.

The more often the business demonstrates the idea, the more substantial it becomes in the customer's mind.

### 10. Durable And Ownable

Can you keep using the idea for years, and can people come to associate it with your business?

A temporary trend may generate attention without giving you something to build on. A completely generic statement may last forever while never becoming associated with anyone in particular.

We want an idea with enough substance to support repeated explanation, stories, and proof.

There is also a difference between an idea with the potential to become associated with you and one you already own in the audience's mind.

The latter takes time and consistent work. A new business can have a strong candidate without pretending that association already exists.

### Three Examples Of How This Works

The examples in the course show different kinds of Big Ideas. They are my interpretations of the marketing, used to explain the framework.

You do not need to copy their phrases. Look at the relationship between the idea, the audience, the content, and the offer.

### Shelby Sapp: Girls Can Be In Sales

The central idea in this example is that women can build a successful career in sales.

That speaks directly to an audience interested in a different income and career opportunity. It also connects to identity and what someone believes is possible for them.

The idea becomes concrete when the marketing shows women taking calls, doing the work, and discussing their experiences.

Training people to work in sales then makes sense as the offer. The content introduces and demonstrates the opportunity; the offer helps them learn the skill required to participate.

This is a useful example of a simple idea supporting many different pieces of marketing. A personal story, a student result, a sales lesson, and a discussion of career choices can all reinforce it.

In the framework, I rate this example highly because the customer, idea, proof, and offer fit together. The lesson is that fit, rather than any single headline.

### Jeremy Haynes: $1 Million Per Month

This example centers the marketing on a clear target: $1 million per month.

The number is concrete and easy to remember. For an ambitious established business owner, it can change the scale of what they are considering.

Advertising, sales, hiring, teams, and systems can all be discussed in relation to what a company needs to reach that level.

That gives different subjects a common direction. It also makes an offer about building those capabilities easy to understand.

The target on its own would be a claim. Business breakdowns, member stories, and relevant evidence are what give someone a reason to take it seriously.

This is a good example of how repetition creates association. The same target can appear throughout the marketing while the individual lessons and examples change.

### De Beers: A Diamond Is Forever

This example connects a physical product with an existing emotional idea: permanent commitment.

The phrase is short and easy to repeat. The product gives the abstract idea a concrete object.

Proposals, weddings, and stories about couples provide many ways to express the same association. The marketing can change its characters and setting while reinforcing the central meaning.

The important point is that it gives the product a reason to matter beyond its physical description.

You can understand why it belongs in the customer's decision about a symbol of commitment. The connection between the idea and the product is immediate.
